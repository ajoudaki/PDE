# Part I. Original source notes

This part preserves the original raw proposal in lightly normalized Markdown. Spelling and display formatting have been cleaned where necessary, but the conceptual content has not been changed. The original LaTeX NTK sample is summarized here and preserved as a source snapshot in Appendix A.

## 1. Baseline setup

Use a canonical multilayer perceptron with preactivation

$$
z^\ell=W^\ell h^{\ell-1},
\qquad
h^\ell=\phi(z^\ell),
\qquad
h^0=x,
\qquad
f(x)=z^L.
$$

For a single sample $(x,y)$, use the loss

$$
\mathcal L(x,y)=(f(x)-y)^2.
$$

Initialization and scaling are intended to follow the μP mean-field feature-learning setup. For convenience, all hidden layers may have width $n$, and the mean-field limit is $n\to\infty$.

## 2. Group $\ell$

Suppose $z^\ell$ is the preactivation generated by the linear weights $W^\ell$, with activation $\phi$ applied to it. Any object that is a $W^\ell$ term, a $z^\ell$ term, a $\phi(z^\ell)$ term, a higher activation derivative, or another scalar function of $z^\ell$, belongs to group $\ell$.

## 3. Explicit terms

The terms above involve direct weights, preactivations, and postactivations with scalar activation functions such as $\phi$, $\phi'$, and $\phi''$. These are called explicit group-$\ell$ terms.

An object such as the backward error

$$
\delta^\ell=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z^\ell}
$$

is implicit rather than explicit. Chain rules, product rules, and higher-order derivative rules can expand any finite-order derivative object into a sum of products of explicit terms. The individual products are called product terms.

## 4. Scalarization

Product terms are normally matrix or higher-order tensor contractions and are therefore noncommutative before their indices are exposed. Any tensor contraction can be written in scalar form by introducing enough indices. For example,

$$
(AB)_{ij}=\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}.
$$

After doing this across a product term, every matrix or tensor product becomes an ordinary scalar product together with explicit index sums. The resulting expressions are scalarized product terms. They involve only scalar weight entries, scalar preactivation entries, and scalar functions of preactivations.

Because the goal is an initialization expectation, linearity allows the expectation to be applied term by term.

## 5. Grouping and reordering from bottom to top

Within a scalarized product term, scalar multiplication is commutative. Terms can therefore be grouped by layer and ordered from the lowest group index to the highest, with the highest group placed at the far right.

## 6. Pushing the top-group expectation forward

Suppose the highest active group is $m$. The intended step is to integrate the fresh randomness of $W^m$ only after all lower-group factors have been placed outside that expectation. Informally, lower-layer randomness is separate from the fresh randomness in $W^m$.

## 7. Mean-field multivariate Gaussian limit

The forward pass produces layerwise Gram matrices transported by Gaussian expectations. Schematically,

$$
G^{\ell+1}
=
\mathbb E\bigl[\phi(X)\phi(Y)^\top\bigr],
\qquad
(X,Y)\sim N(0,G^\ell).
$$

In the mean-field limit, each neuron row of preactivations is described by a deterministic covariance matrix. Distinct rows are independent. Weight elements are independent unless their indices agree. Weights and same-row preactivations possess a small covariance controlled by the width scaling.

The intended result is that the relevant top-layer weight and preactivation coordinates can be treated through a prescribed joint Gaussian structure.

## 8. Wick/Isserlis contractions

If a product contains only linear Gaussian terms, Wick's or Isserlis' theorem converts the expectation of the product into a sum over pairings.

## 9. Stein/Onsager corrections

If nonlinear functions of preactivations occur, Stein's lemma is used to separate the weight–preactivation dependence. Informally, this produces a pseudo-independent contribution plus correction terms involving derivatives of the nonlinear functions and fewer explicit weight factors.

One product term may therefore become a sum of simpler product terms.

## 10. Weight–activation pseudo-independence terms

Applying the Stein/Onsager step inductively to the highest group is intended to produce terms in which top-layer weights can be integrated separately from top-layer activation-related factors, together with explicit correction branches.

## 11. Wick contractions on the remaining weight elements

Once the weight dependence has been isolated, Wick/Isserlis contractions remove the remaining weights. Some contraction patterns vanish and others survive, producing Kronecker or Dirac delta constraints among neuron indices.

## 12. Mean-field peeling

After the top-layer weights have been removed, the remaining nonconstant top-group objects are functions of top-layer preactivations. The original scalarization contains large index sums. Wick pairings break these sums into a small number of equality patterns.

For example,

$$
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\phi(z_i)\phi(z_j)
=
\left(\frac1n\sum_i\phi(z_i)\right)^2
\longrightarrow
\bigl(\mathbb E\phi(Z)\bigr)^2,
$$

whereas an enforced equality pattern gives

$$
\frac1n\sum_i\phi(z_i)^2
\longrightarrow
\mathbb E[\phi(Z)^2].
$$

Each equality pattern defines a family of sums with its own mean-field limit and width scaling. After evaluating these sums, the entire effect of the top group is replaced by scalar Gaussian expectations. All remaining stochastic terms involve lower groups only.

Thus an expectation of scalarized products with highest group $m$ is reduced to a weighted sum of expectations of scalarized products with highest group at most $m-1$. This is the mean-field peeling step.

## 13. Repeated peeling

Repeat the same operation one group at a time. Eventually every term is reduced to products of explicit Gaussian expectations of the form

$$
\mathbb E\left[f_1(Z_1)f_2(Z_2)\cdots f_r(Z_r)\right],
$$

where the covariance of the preactivations is determined by the layerwise forward Gram matrix and the functions are identity, $\phi$, $\phi'$, $\phi''$, or higher derivatives.

For a fixed batch of size $B$, the primitive Gaussian integration is intended to be finite-dimensional and controlled by the fixed batch covariance matrices.

## 14. Practical tips from the original proposal

### 14.1 Retain only leading width patterns

Only leading surviving width patterns matter for a leading-order mean-field result. A pattern with fewer free assignments may be neglected if it cannot recover the lost width order in later lower-layer summations.

The phrase “contribute similarly downstream” is important: local top-layer counts alone are insufficient if two patterns create different lower-layer sums.

### 14.2 Recursive scalarization

Full scalarization of every layer at once may be prohibitively complex. Instead, expose and scalarize only the current top group. After it is peeled, repeat on the next group.

### 14.3 Recursive states and dynamic programming

The equality patterns and lower-layer boundary signatures created by one peel may close on a finite family of states. If so, the computation can be written as a recursive or dynamic-programming system.

## 15. Original headline claim

The original intended headline was:

> Any object involving derivatives or higher-order derivatives at initialization of a depth-$L$ MLP in the mean-field regime, with arbitrary but constant $O(1)$ maximum derivative order and a fixed batch of $B$ samples, can be computed through a set of $O(L)$ recursive equations involving only explicit Gaussian expectations of layerwise preactivations passed through identity, $\phi$, $\phi'$, $\phi''$, and higher derivatives.

## 16. Original feature-learning motivation

Under a μP mean-field feature-learning parameterization, the change in a hidden-layer Gram matrix after one step can be expanded to fixed order in the learning rate using higher derivatives of the network at initialization. At later steps, gradient corrections can similarly be expanded using higher derivatives.

In the gradient-flow scaling

$$
t\to\infty,
\qquad
\eta\to0,
\qquad
t\eta=\tau,
$$

the coefficients become time derivatives of the training trajectory at initialization. Each such coefficient is intended to be an object handled by the peeling program.

## 17. Original conjecture

> For a neural network evaluated on fixed inputs and possessing a well-defined mean-field learning limit, any scalar observable such as loss or a hidden-layer Gram matrix, provided its training curve is locally smooth, has initialization derivatives expressible as sums of products of explicit Gaussian integrals. Their covariance matrices are finite and determined by the input, output, architecture, initialization, and loss configuration.

## 18. Role of the original NTK sample

The supplied LaTeX note attempted a first-principles NTK derivation using forward covariance recursions, backward Jacobian moments, Wick contractions, and Stein corrections. Its intended lessons were:

- forward preactivation covariances obey a deterministic Gaussian recursion;
- backward influence can be propagated recursively;
- the classical NTK is built from forward activation kernels and derivative-transport factors;
- diagonal and off-diagonal backward patterns must be distinguished;
- off-diagonal corrections should be checked rather than discarded by an independence assumption.

The sample is a source of intuition, not yet an adopted proof. Its notation changes weight-index conventions, switches from square loss to a linear-output surrogate, and leaves several second-moment and off-diagonal states incompletely defined.

# Part II. Current maintained understanding

## 19. The central mathematical operation

The current best formulation is:

> Mean-field peeling is layerwise conditional Gaussian elimination of scalar contraction diagrams.

It is not based on global Gaussianity and is not based on unconditional independence between a weight matrix and the preactivations generated by that matrix.

The operation has four exact components:

1. expose the current highest-layer contraction;
2. condition on all preceding layers;
3. remove the fresh Gaussian variables using Wick and Stein identities;
4. convert top-neuron row sums into Gaussian moments while passing the remaining contraction state to the next lower layer.

## 20. Notation and scaling ledger

For the maintained formulation, let

$$
A^\ell_{ij}=s_{\ell,n}\theta^\ell_{ij}
$$

denote the effective weight used in the forward pass, where $\theta^\ell$ is the raw parameter coordinate. Write

$$
z_i^\ell(a)
=
\sum_j A^\ell_{ij}h_j^{\ell-1}(a)
+\sigma_{b,\ell}b_i^\ell.
$$

For fan-in initialization,

$$
A^\ell_{ij}\sim N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{w,\ell}^2}{n_{\ell-1}}\right).
$$

A complete peeling problem must declare:

- all widths and their limiting ratios;
- the variance and multiplier of each effective weight;
- the raw parameter with respect to which derivatives are taken;
- output dimension and readout scaling;
- bias scaling;
- layerwise optimizer or μP metric factors;
- the target normalization and desired asymptotic order;
- whether the target is an expectation, a deterministic limit, or a fluctuation law.

No width power counting is meaningful before this ledger is fixed.

## 21. Exact layerwise conditional Gaussian law

Let

$$
\mathcal F_{\ell-1}
=
\sigma(A^1,b^1,\ldots,A^{\ell-1},b^{\ell-1}).
$$

Conditional on $\mathcal F_{\ell-1}$, the row blocks

$$
\bigl(A^\ell_{i,:},b_i^\ell,Z_i^\ell\bigr),
\qquad
Z_i^\ell=(z_i^\ell(1),\ldots,z_i^\ell(B)),
$$

are independent centered jointly Gaussian vectors. Exactly at finite width,

$$
Q^\ell_{n,ab}
:=
\operatorname{Cov}(z_i^\ell(a),z_i^\ell(b)\mid\mathcal F_{\ell-1})
=
\frac{\sigma_{w,\ell}^2}{n_{\ell-1}}
\sum_jh_j^{\ell-1}(a)h_j^{\ell-1}(b)
+\sigma_{b,\ell}^2,
$$

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(A^\ell_{ij},A^\ell_{i'j'})
=
\delta_{ii'}\delta_{jj'}
\frac{\sigma_{w,\ell}^2}{n_{\ell-1}},
$$

and

$$
\operatorname{Cov}
\left(A^\ell_{ij},z_{i'}^\ell(a)\mid\mathcal F_{\ell-1}\right)
=
\delta_{ii'}
\frac{\sigma_{w,\ell}^2}{n_{\ell-1}}
h_j^{\ell-1}(a).
$$

This conditional statement replaces the original global joint-Gaussian statement. Unconditionally, the covariance contains random lower-layer activations, so the joint law across multiple layers is generally a Gaussian mixture rather than a single Gaussian vector.

## 22. Deterministic forward state

Under standard law-of-large-numbers and moment assumptions,

$$
Q_n^\ell\longrightarrow Q^\ell,
$$

where

$$
Q^1_{ab}
=
\sigma_{w,1}^2\frac{(x^a)^\top x^b}{n_0}
+\sigma_{b,1}^2,
$$

and

$$
Q^{\ell+1}_{ab}
=
\sigma_{w,\ell+1}^2
\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q^\ell)}
[\phi_\ell(Z_a)\phi_\ell(Z_b)]
+\sigma_{b,\ell+1}^2.
$$

Labels do not enter these initialization covariances. They enter through loss derivatives and training-time vector fields.

## 23. Explicit atoms and admissible observables

An explicit group-$\ell$ atom is:

- an effective or raw layer-$\ell$ parameter coordinate;
- a bias coordinate;
- a preactivation coordinate $z_i^\ell(a)$;
- a scalar function $\psi(z_i^\ell(a))$, including activation and loss derivatives.

The initial admissible observable class is:

> Width-normalized scalar contractions of finitely many parameter, input, or training-time derivatives of the network, loss, or a smooth permutation-invariant observable, evaluated on a fixed batch at initialization.

This class deliberately requires a scalar contraction. An uncontracted parameter Hessian with a width-growing number of free indices is not itself one scalar peeling state.

Ratios, inverses, maxima, spectral edges, and other global nonlinear functions require additional nondegeneracy or regularity arguments even when their ingredients can be peeled.

## 24. Scalar contraction normal form

After chain-rule, product-rule, and Faà di Bruno expansion, each scalar term takes the form

$$
T_n
=
c_n
\sum_{\boldsymbol\iota}
\mathbf 1\{\mathcal C(\boldsymbol\iota)\}
\prod_{r=1}^{R_W}A^{\ell_r}_{i_rj_r}
\prod_{s=1}^{R_Z}
\psi_s(z^{k_s}_{u_s}(a_s)).
$$

The state records:

- all neuron indices and their layers;
- all fixed batch labels;
- equality and inequality constraints;
- the derivative order of each scalar function;
- all explicit powers of width;
- all open indices that will be passed to lower layers.

This is the precise object on which peeling operates.

## 25. Exact generalized Wick–Stein identity

Let $X_1,\ldots,X_r$ be explicit Gaussian parameter coordinates in the current top group and let $F(Z)$ be the product of all current top-preactivation functions. Conditional on the lower filtration,

$$
\mathbb E\left[\prod_{q=1}^rX_qF(Z)\right]
=
\sum_{\pi}
\left(
\prod_{\{q,t\}\in\pi}\operatorname{Cov}(X_q,X_t)
\right)
\sum_{\alpha:U(\pi)\to\mathcal I_Z}
\left(
\prod_{q\in U(\pi)}
\operatorname{Cov}(X_q,Z_{\alpha(q)})
\right)
\mathbb E\left[
\partial_{\alpha(U(\pi))}F(Z)
\right].
\tag{25.1}
$$

The outer sum is over partial matchings $\pi$ of the weight occurrences, and $U(\pi)$ is the unmatched set.

- A matched pair is a weight–weight Wick contraction.
- An unmatched weight is attached to a preactivation through Stein's identity.
- Every attachment differentiates the nonlinear top factor and inserts a lower-layer activation through the weight–preactivation covariance.

This exact finite identity is the correct replacement for the informal statement that Stein's lemma creates an independent copy plus one correction.

The complete matchings are the pseudo-independent terms. Partial matchings are the Onsager branches. Whether Onsager branches survive is decided by global power counting, not by terminology.

## 26. Equality partitions and Gaussian row moments

For top-layer row indices $i_1,\ldots,i_q$, decompose the sum by exact equality partition:

$$
\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_q}
=
\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal P_q}
\sum_{\ker(\boldsymbol i)=\pi}.
$$

A partition with $|\pi|$ distinct row blocks has

$$
n_\ell^{|\pi|}(1+O(n_\ell^{-1}))
$$

assignments, subject to width ratios and existing constraints.

For one row block, define

$$
\Gamma_\ell
[(a_1,\psi_1),\ldots,(a_s,\psi_s);Q]
:=
\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q)}
\left[\prod_{r=1}^s\psi_r(Z_{a_r})\right].
$$

Factors on distinct top rows become products of such moments. Thus each primitive integral is at most $B$-dimensional, although a diagram may contain several independent row blocks.

## 27. Global width power counting

The width degree of a diagram includes:

- one positive width power for each free neuron-index block;
- negative powers contributed by weight covariances;
- negative powers contributed by weight–preactivation attachments;
- the observable normalization;
- raw-to-effective parameter multipliers;
- μP learning-rate or metric factors;
- any lower-layer sums created by Stein attachments.

A pattern may be discarded only after its entire lower-layer boundary signature has been accounted for. Locally subleading terms can become comparable if they create additional lower-layer sums, while locally comparable terms can become subleading when they must later satisfy an extra equality.

For fluctuation observables, subleading diagrams may be the target and must not be discarded.

## 28. Replacing empirical covariance by deterministic covariance

After exact top-layer integration, coefficients initially depend on the lower-random empirical covariance $Q_n^\ell$. For a uniformly integrable leading-order state,

$$
Q_n^\ell\to Q^\ell
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\Gamma_\ell[\cdots;Q_n^\ell]
\to
\Gamma_\ell[\cdots;Q^\ell].
$$

For centered or fluctuation-amplified states, the zeroth-order replacement may erase the leading term. One then expands in $Q_n^\ell-Q^\ell$. Price's identity keeps the expansion within the same Gaussian derivative algebra:

$$
D\,\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q)}[F(Z)][H]
=
\frac12\sum_{a,b}H_{ab}
\mathbb E[\partial_{ab}F(Z)],
$$

with the standard symmetric-covariance convention.

## 29. Formal recursive algorithm

### Inputs

1. Architecture, widths, and activation functions.
2. Effective and raw parameter scalings.
3. Output and bias normalization.
4. Optimizer or μP metric scaling when training derivatives are involved.
5. Fixed input-label batch.
6. Scalar normalized observable.
7. Maximum derivative order.
8. Desired width order and convergence mode.

### One peeling pass

1. Expand only enough derivative rules to expose the current highest group.
2. Scalarize the top-group contractions.
3. Record exact width powers and lower-layer boundary indices.
4. Reorder scalar atoms by group.
5. Let $p$ be the highest active group.
6. Condition on $\mathcal F_{p-1}$.
7. Partition top-row indices into compatible equality patterns.
8. Apply the generalized Wick–Stein identity until no explicit group-$p$ Gaussian parameter remains.
9. Expand every differentiated activation product.
10. Substitute exact conditional covariances.
11. Resolve Kronecker deltas and update the lower-layer boundary state.
12. Perform global width power counting.
13. Convert surviving top-row functions to Gaussian moments at $Q_n^p$.
14. Replace $Q_n^p$ by $Q^p$, or retain the necessary covariance expansion.
15. Combine algebraically identical lower states.
16. Repeat at group $p-1$.
17. At layer zero, evaluate deterministic input and label contractions.

### Convergence pass

If the result is claimed to hold for a typical initialization rather than only in expectation, repeat the procedure for the square, covariance, or relevant cumulants and prove concentration.

## 30. Dynamic-programming form

A recursive state stores only the active open contraction legs, derivative orders, batch labels, equality partitions, lower-layer constraints, and accumulated width degree.

For a finite state family $\mathcal S$, one peel has the form

$$
V_p(s)
=
\sum_{s'\in\mathcal S}
T_p(s,s';Q^p)V_{p-1}(s'),
$$

where each transition entry is a finite sum of scaling constants and Gaussian moments.

Thus

$$
V_L=T_L(Q^L)\cdots T_1(Q^1)V_0.
$$

The number of layer transitions is $O(L)$. The state dimension can depend combinatorially on derivative order, batch size, observable type, and requested asymptotic order. Closure on a state space independent of depth is a proof obligation, not an automatic consequence of fixed derivative order.

## 31. Current programmatic claim

The strongest claim currently justified as a program specification, rather than a theorem, is:

> Fix a batch size $B$, derivative order $R$, depth $L$, and a Gaussian fully connected architecture whose hidden widths tend to infinity with fixed positive ratios. Let $O_n$ be an $O(1)$-normalized admissible scalar contraction of the initialization jet of order at most $R$. Assume sufficient activation smoothness and moment bounds, deterministic convergence of the forward empirical Gram matrices, uniform integrability of all retained states, and finite-state closure of the layerwise contraction rules. Then the leading large-width expectation of $O_n$ is computable through $L$ layer-local peeling transitions. Every transition coefficient is a finite linear combination of explicit batch-dimensional Gaussian expectations of scalar activation functions and their derivatives, evaluated at deterministic layerwise preactivation covariances. If second-moment peeling proves vanishing variance, the same expression is the limit in probability.

This remains a theorem schema until the admissible class, required regularity, state closure, normalization classes, and convergence arguments are proved.

## 32. Important current qualifications

1. The width limit presently assumes fixed depth, batch size, and derivative order. Depth growing with width requires new uniform estimates.
2. “Any scalar observable with a mean-field limit” is too broad without an admissibility condition.
3. Expectation, convergence in probability, and convergence in distribution are distinct outputs.
4. A scalar output layer has no neuron-row law-of-large-numbers factor and must be handled separately.
5. ReLU requires smoothing, weak derivatives, or distributional integration by parts.
6. Bias–preactivation covariances can be $O(1)$; bias Stein branches are not automatically negligible.
7. The activation derivative order required by peeling may exceed the nominal derivative order of the original observable.
8. A noncentered readout can require backward first-moment and off-diagonal states.
9. A zero-initialized readout can make the initialization backward kernel vanish, moving the first nontrivial state to a training-time derivative.

# Part III. Feature-learning interface

## 33. Gradient-flow derivatives

Let the scaled gradient flow be

$$
\dot\theta_n(\tau)
=
-P_n\nabla_\theta\mathcal R_n(\theta_n(\tau)),
$$

where $P_n$ is the layerwise optimizer metric or μP preconditioner. Define the differential operator

$$
\mathcal V_n
:=
-\bigl(P_n\nabla\mathcal R_n\bigr)\cdot\nabla.
$$

For a smooth scalar observable $A_n$,

$$
\frac{d^r}{d\tau^r}A_n(\theta_n(\tau))\bigg|_{\tau=0}
=
\mathcal V_n^rA_n(\theta_n(0)).
\tag{33.1}
$$

At fixed $r$, repeated application of $\mathcal V_n$ creates a finite sum of scalar contractions of derivatives of $A_n$, the network, and the loss at initialization. These are peeling inputs.

## 34. Hidden feature kernels

For example,

$$
K^\ell_{n,ab}(\tau)
=
\frac1{n_\ell}
\sum_i
h_i^\ell(x^a;\theta_\tau)
h_i^\ell(x^b;\theta_\tau).
$$

The intended feature-learning calculation is

$$
\frac{d^r}{d\tau^r}K^\ell_{n,ab}(0)
\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}
\text{a finite sum of products of layerwise Gaussian moments}.
$$

The logical chain is

$$
\text{training-time derivative}
\longrightarrow
\text{initialization derivative contractions}
\longrightarrow
\text{peeling states}
\longrightarrow
\text{explicit Gaussian moments}.
$$

## 35. What this does and does not yet establish

The program computes fixed-order initialization jets. It does not by itself prove:

- interchange of the infinite-width limit with time differentiation;
- convergence or concentration of every coefficient;
- a nonzero radius of convergence of the time-Taylor series;
- recovery of a finite-time training trajectory from its local jet;
- validity of a fixed-step learning-rate expansion when the number of steps scales like $1/\eta$.

These are separate analytic obligations.

# Part IV. Worked execution: the backward sensitivity kernel

## 36. Definition and normalization

“Backward kernel” is taken here to mean the output-sensitivity kernel. Let hidden layers be $1,\ldots,H$, where $H=L-1$, all of width $n$. Use hidden effective weights

$$
W^\ell_{ij}\sim N\left(0,\frac{s_\ell^2}{n}\right).
$$

Write the scalar readout as

$$
f_a
=
s_Ln^{-\alpha}
\sum_{i=1}^na_i h_i^H(a),
\qquad
a_i\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}N(0,1).
$$

The choices

$$
\alpha=\frac12
$$

and

$$
\alpha=1
$$

represent, respectively, a standard random-output/NTK-size readout and a common mean-field/μP-size readout.

Define

$$
g_i^{\ell,a}
:=
\frac{\partial f_a}{\partial z_i^\ell(a)},
\qquad
\beta_i^{\ell,a}:=n^\alpha g_i^{\ell,a}.
$$

Then

$$
\beta_i^{H,a}
=
s_La_i\phi'(z_i^{H,a}),
\tag{36.1}
$$

and, for $\ell<H$,

$$
\beta_i^{\ell,a}
=
\phi'(z_i^{\ell,a})
\sum_{r=1}^nW^{\ell+1}_{ri}\beta_r^{\ell+1,a}.
\tag{36.2}
$$

The normalized backward kernel is

$$
\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab}
:=
\frac1n\sum_i
\beta_i^{\ell,a}\beta_i^{\ell,b}
=
n^{2\alpha-1}
\sum_i g_i^{\ell,a}g_i^{\ell,b}.
\tag{36.3}
$$

For $\alpha=1/2$, this is the ordinary backward inner product. For $\alpha=1$, the ordinary inner product is $n^{-1}\widehat\Pi^\ell$.

## 37. Gaussian derivative coefficients

Let

$$
Z^\ell\sim N(0,Q^\ell)
$$

and define

$$
D^\ell_{ab}
:=
\mathbb E[\phi'(Z^\ell_a)\phi'(Z^\ell_b)].
\tag{37.1}
$$

For hidden transitions, define

$$
\dot Q^\ell_{ab}
:=
s_{\ell+1}^2D^\ell_{ab}.
$$

## 38. Boundary peel

At the last hidden layer,

$$
\widehat\Pi^H_{n,ab}
=
\frac{s_L^2}{n}
\sum_i
a_i^2
\phi'(z_i^{H,a})
\phi'(z_i^{H,b}).
$$

The readout variables are independent of $z^H$, so a weighted law of large numbers gives

$$
\widehat\Pi^H_{n,ab}
\longrightarrow
s_L^2D^H_{ab}.
\tag{38.1}
$$

This is a pure readout Wick contraction followed by a rowwise Gaussian expectation.

## 39. Recursive scalarization

Let

$$
u_i^{\ell,ab}
:=
\phi'(z_i^{\ell,a})
\phi'(z_i^{\ell,b}).
$$

Substituting the backward recursion into the kernel gives the exact scalar expression

$$
\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab}
=
\frac1n
\sum_{i,r,s}
u_i^{\ell,ab}
W^{\ell+1}_{ri}W^{\ell+1}_{si}
\beta_r^{\ell+1,a}\beta_s^{\ell+1,b}.
\tag{39.1}
$$

This is recursive scalarization: only the current weight layer has been exposed, while the upper backward state remains packaged as a function of upper preactivations.

## 40. Exact finite-width Wick–Stein peel

Set

$$
v_{\ell+1}=\frac{s_{\ell+1}^2}{n}.
$$

For a smooth function $F$ of $W^{\ell+1}$, Gaussian integration by parts gives

$$
\mathbb E[W^{\ell+1}_{ri}W^{\ell+1}_{si}F]
=
v_{\ell+1}\delta_{rs}\mathbb E[F]
+v_{\ell+1}^2
\mathbb E[\partial_{W^{\ell+1}_{ri}}
\partial_{W^{\ell+1}_{si}}F].
\tag{40.1}
$$

Apply this with

$$
F=\beta_r^{\ell+1,a}\beta_s^{\ell+1,b}.
$$

Because $W^{\ell+1}_{ri}$ influences the upper network through the batch preactivation vector of neuron $r$,

$$
\partial_{W^{\ell+1}_{ri}}
=
\sum_{c=1}^B
h_i^{\ell,c}
\partial_{z_r^{\ell+1,c}}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\mathbb E_{W^{\ell+1}}[\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab}]
=
s_{\ell+1}^2
\widehat D^\ell_{n,ab}
\mathbb E_{W^{\ell+1}}[\widehat\Pi^{\ell+1}_{n,ab}]
+R^\ell_{n,ab},
\tag{40.2}
$$

where

$$
\widehat D^\ell_{n,ab}
=
\frac1n\sum_i u_i^{\ell,ab}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
R^\ell_{n,ab}
&=
\frac{s_{\ell+1}^4}{n^3}
\sum_{i,r,s}
\sum_{c,d=1}^B
u_i^{\ell,ab}
h_i^{\ell,c}h_i^{\ell,d}
\\
&\qquad\qquad\times
\mathbb E\left[
\partial_{z_r^{\ell+1,c}}
\partial_{z_s^{\ell+1,d}}
\bigl(
\beta_r^{\ell+1,a}
\beta_s^{\ell+1,b}
\bigr)
\right].
\end{aligned}
\tag{40.3}
$$

Equation (40.2) is exact at finite width after the stated conditional expectation. It displays both the pseudo-independent Wick branch and the Onsager remainder.

## 41. First nontrivial explicit correction

Consider the transition from layer $H-1$ to layer $H$. Integrating the centered readout gives

$$
\mathbb E[a_ra_s]=\delta_{rs}.
$$

Let

$$
\psi_{ab}(Z)=\phi'(Z_a)\phi'(Z_b).
$$

Conditional on $\mathcal F_{H-1}$,

$$
\operatorname{Cov}(W^H_{ri},z_r^{H,c}\mid\mathcal F_{H-1})
=
\frac{s_H^2}{n}h_i^{H-1,c}.
$$

Hence

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[(W^H_{ri})^2\psi_{ab}(Z_r^H)\mid\mathcal F_{H-1}]
&=
\frac{s_H^2}{n}
\mathbb E[\psi_{ab}(Z)]
\\
&\quad+
\frac{s_H^4}{n^2}
\sum_{c,d=1}^B
h_i^{H-1,c}h_i^{H-1,d}
\mathbb E[\partial_{cd}\psi_{ab}(Z)],
\end{aligned}
\tag{41.1}
$$

where $Z\sim N(0,Q_n^H)$.

Substitution yields

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[\widehat\Pi^{H-1}_{n,ab}\mid\mathcal F_{H-1}]
&=
s_H^2\widehat D^{H-1}_{n,ab}
\left[
s_L^2
\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q_n^H)}
[\phi'(Z_a)\phi'(Z_b)]
\right]
\\
&\quad+
\frac{s_L^2s_H^4}{n^2}
\sum_i u_i^{H-1,ab}
\sum_{c,d=1}^B
h_i^{H-1,c}h_i^{H-1,d}
\mathbb E[\partial_{cd}\psi_{ab}(Z)].
\end{aligned}
\tag{41.2}
$$

The second line is the explicit Onsager correction. It is $O(n^{-1})$ for fixed batch size and bounded moments.

For $a\ne b$, its Gaussian integrands are combinations of

$$
\phi'''(Z_a)\phi'(Z_b),
\qquad
\phi''(Z_a)\phi''(Z_b),
\qquad
\phi'(Z_a)\phi'''(Z_b).
$$

The leading term gives

$$
\Pi^{H-1}_{ab}
=
s_H^2D^{H-1}_{ab}
s_L^2D^H_{ab}.
$$

## 42. Two-path diagram and leading ladder pattern

Unrolling backpropagation gives

$$
\beta_{i_\ell}^{\ell,a}
=
s_L
\sum_{i_{\ell+1},\ldots,i_H}
a_{i_H}
\left(
\prod_{t=\ell}^H
\phi'(z_{i_t}^{t,a})
\right)
\left(
\prod_{t=\ell+1}^H
W^t_{i_ti_{t-1}}
\right).
\tag{42.1}
$$

The kernel contains two such paths, one for sample $a$ and one for sample $b$.

The leading equality pattern is the ladder:

1. The readout contraction forces the two terminal indices to agree.
2. At each lower hidden layer, the weight–weight contraction forces the two path indices to agree.
3. Each shared hidden index contributes $n$ choices.
4. Each paired hidden weight contributes a covariance factor $n^{-1}$.
5. These powers cancel layer by layer.
6. The paired activation derivatives average to $D^t_{ab}$.

A Stein attachment may release one of these Wick equalities and produce an extra free index, but it also carries an extra inverse-width covariance. Because the two paths share their bottom index in the kernel definition, any split must eventually reconnect or satisfy an additional equality, losing a width factor.

For a centered readout, a nominally leading disconnected split also vanishes because each isolated backward branch has zero first moment. Thus

$$
R^\ell_{n,ab}=O(n^{-1})
$$

in expectation for fixed depth under the stated regularity conditions.

This is the precise pseudo-independence result for this observable. It is not a universal statement about all backward or higher-derivative observables.

## 43. Backward recursion

The deterministic limiting recursion is

$$
\boxed{\Pi^H=s_L^2D^H}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
\Pi^\ell
=
s_{\ell+1}^2D^\ell\odot\Pi^{\ell+1},
\qquad
1\le\ell<H,
}
\tag{43.1}
$$

where $\odot$ is entrywise multiplication.

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
\Pi^\ell_{ab}
=
\left(\prod_{r=\ell+1}^{L}s_r^2\right)
\left(\prod_{t=\ell}^{H}D^t_{ab}\right).
}
\tag{43.2}
$$

This is a one-state, $O(L)$ execution of the peeling program.

## 44. Concentration

To pass from the expected recursion to a deterministic kernel limit, expand the second moment. Four backward paths appear. The leading disconnected pair of ladders reproduces

$$
(\mathbb E\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab})^2.
$$

Every connected four-path pattern loses at least one free neuron index. Under standard fixed-depth moment assumptions,

$$
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab})=O(n^{-1}),
$$

so

$$
\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab}\to\Pi^\ell_{ab}
\qquad\text{in }L^2.
$$

The dropped deterministic Stein bias is typically $O(n^{-1})$, whereas finite-width random fluctuations are typically $O_p(n^{-1/2})$.

## 45. Square-loss adjoint

For

$$
\mathcal L_a=(f_a-y_a)^2,
\qquad
\rho_a=2(f_a-y_a),
$$

the normalized loss adjoint is

$$
n^\alpha\frac{\partial\mathcal L_a}{\partial z_i^\ell(a)}
=
\rho_a\beta_i^{\ell,a}.
$$

Therefore its backward kernel obeys the exact identity

$$
\widehat\Pi^{\ell,\mathrm{loss}}_{n,ab}
=
4(f_a-y_a)(f_b-y_b)
\widehat\Pi^\ell_{n,ab}.
\tag{45.1}
$$

For a centered mean-field readout with $\alpha=1$, $f_a\to0$, giving

$$
\widehat\Pi^{\ell,\mathrm{loss}}_{n,ab}
\to
4y_ay_b\Pi^\ell_{ab}.
$$

For $\alpha=1/2$, the output has a nondegenerate Gaussian-process limit $F$, and the loss kernel has the generally random limit

$$
4(F_a-y_a)(F_b-y_b)\Pi^\ell_{ab}.
$$

If $F$ is centered with covariance $K^f$, its annealed expectation is

$$
4(K^f_{ab}+y_ay_b)\Pi^\ell_{ab}.
$$

## 46. Relation to the NTK sample

The standard backward kernel $\Pi^\ell$ is a second-moment object. It should not be conflated with the mixed first moment $M_{k,\ell}$ in the original sample.

With a fully specified parameter coordinate and optimizer metric, the layer-$\ell$ NTK contribution has the schematic form

$$
\text{forward activation Gram at layer }\ell-1
\quad\odot\quad
\Pi^\ell,
$$

times the relevant parameter-scaling factor. This distinction explains why the direct NTK term contains a forward activation covariance, while backward transport contributes the derivative factors.

## 46A. Complete concrete execution through groups 4, 3, 2, and 1

This section records the fully scalarized four-layer example used in the discussion. It uses hidden groups $1,2,3$ and a scalar readout group $4$, all hidden widths being $n$. There is no extra depth symbol in this calculation.

For two fixed inputs $x,x'$, let

$$
z_p^2(u)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_a W^2_{pa}h_a^1(u),
\qquad
z_r^3(u)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_p W^3_{rp}h_p^2(u),
$$

$$
h_i^\ell(u)=\phi(z_i^\ell(u)),
\qquad
z^4(u)=\frac1n\sum_rW_r^4h_r^3(u),
$$

where every displayed raw weight is independent $N(0,1)$. Assume $\phi\in C^3$ and that all Gaussian and empirical moments used below are uniformly integrable. Define

$$
\delta_i^\ell(u)=n\frac{\partial z^4(u)}{\partial z_i^\ell(u)},
\qquad
B_n^1(x,x')=\frac1n\sum_a\delta_a^1(x)\delta_a^1(x').
$$

The chain rule gives

$$
\delta_r^3(u)=W_r^4\phi'(z_r^3(u)),
$$

$$
\delta_p^2(u)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\phi'(z_p^2(u))
\sum_rW^3_{rp}\delta_r^3(u),
$$

$$
\delta_a^1(u)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\phi'(z_a^1(u))
\sum_pW^2_{pa}\delta_p^2(u).
$$

The exact scalarization is

$$
\begin{aligned}
B_n^1(x,x')
&=\frac1{n^3}\sum_{a,p,q,r,t}
W_r^4W_t^4W^3_{rp}W^3_{tq}W^2_{pa}W^2_{qa}
\\
&\quad\times
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\phi'(z_p^2(x))\phi'(z_q^2(x'))
\phi'(z_r^3(x))\phi'(z_t^3(x')).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.1}
$$

All indices in this section are explicit. The index $r$ belongs only to group 3 after the readout is integrated. The indices $p,q$ also occur in group 2 and therefore cannot be summed out during the group-3 peel. The index $a$ occurs in groups 2 and 1 and must remain until the group-1 peel.

### The exact rowwise Gaussian identities

Let $w_1,\ldots,w_n$ be independent $N(0,1)$ and

$$
Z(u)=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_jw_jh_j(u).
$$

Conditionally on the lower-layer numbers $h_j(u)$, Gaussian integration by parts gives

$$
\mathbb E[w_pG(Z)]
=
\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_u h_p(u)\mathbb E[\partial_uG(Z)]
\tag{46A.2}
$$

and, after applying it twice,

$$
\mathbb E[w_pw_qG(Z)]
=
\delta_{pq}\mathbb E[G(Z)]
+\frac1n\sum_{u,v}h_p(u)h_q(v)
\mathbb E[\partial_u\partial_vG(Z)].
\tag{46A.3}
$$

Here and below every sample index $u,v,s,t$ is summed over $\{x,x'\}$, and $\partial_u$ means differentiation with respect to the Gaussian coordinate $Z(u)$. Equation (46A.3), not an independence claim, is the exact Wick--Stein rule used at each hidden layer.

### Peel group 4

Since the readout weights are independent of all hidden groups,

$$
\mathbb E[W_r^4W_t^4]=\delta_{rt}.
$$

Thus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{W^4}[B_n^1]
&=\frac1{n^3}\sum_{a,p,q,r}
W^3_{rp}W^3_{rq}W^2_{pa}W^2_{qa}
\\
&\quad\times
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\phi'(z_p^2(x))\phi'(z_q^2(x'))
\phi'(z_r^3(x))\phi'(z_r^3(x')).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.4}
$$

Only $r$ is now a top-group-only index.

### Peel group 3

Condition on groups 1 and 2 and apply (46A.3) to row $r$ of $W^3$, with

$$
G(Z)=\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x')).
$$

This gives the exact conditional identity

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{W^3}
[W^3_{rp}W^3_{rq}
\phi'(z_r^3(x))\phi'(z_r^3(x'))]
\\
&\quad=
\delta_{pq}\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q_n^3)}
[\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))]
\\
&\qquad+
\frac1n\sum_{u,v}h_p^2(u)h_q^2(v)
\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,Q_n^3)}[
\partial_u\partial_v
\{\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\}],
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.5}
$$

where, at finite width, the covariance inside $\mathbb E_3$ is

$$
Q_n^3(u,v)=\frac1n\sum_jh_j^2(u)h_j^2(v).
$$

The four possible second derivatives in (46A.5) are

$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
(u,v)&\partial_u\partial_v
\{\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\}\\ \hline
(x,x)&\phi'''(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\\
(x,x')&\phi''(Z(x))\phi''(Z(x'))\\
(x',x)&\phi''(Z(x))\phi''(Z(x'))\\
(x',x')&\phi'(Z(x))\phi'''(Z(x')).
\end{array}
\tag{46A.6}
$$

The factors $h_p^2(u),h_q^2(v)$ created by Stein differentiation belong to group 2, not group 3. They must therefore be retained for the next peel.

For the leading mean-field theorem, replace $Q_n^3$ by its deterministic forward limit

$$
Q^3(u,v)=\mathbb E_2[\phi(Z(u))\phi(Z(v))].
$$

This is justified by the fixed-batch forward law of large numbers, continuity of the displayed Gaussian expectations in the covariance, and uniform integrability. Because the singled-out rows $p,q$ also occur in $Q_n^3$, a fully quantitative proof uses a leave-two-out Gram matrix. The removal changes the covariance by $O(n^{-1})$, while the leave-two-out matrix converges to $Q^3$ and is conditionally independent of rows $p,q$.

For compactness only within this execution, write

$$
D_\ell=\mathbb E_\ell[\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))]
$$

and

$$
C_\ell(u,v)=
\mathbb E_\ell[
\partial_u\partial_v
\{\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\}],
$$

where $\mathbb E_\ell$ uses the deterministic covariance $Q^\ell$. Substitution of (46A.5) into (46A.4) gives two group-2 expressions:

$$
\frac{D_3}{n^2}\sum_{a,p}(W^2_{pa})^2
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\phi'(z_p^2(x))\phi'(z_p^2(x'))
\tag{46A.7}
$$

and

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{n^3}\sum_{a,p,q}W^2_{pa}W^2_{qa}
&\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\\
&\times\sum_{u,v}C_3(u,v)
\phi'(z_p^2(x))\phi(z_p^2(u))
\phi'(z_q^2(x'))\phi(z_q^2(v)).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.8}
$$

### Peel group 2: the branch from the group-3 Wick pairing

Condition on group 1. The exact conditional covariance of a row of $z^2$ is

$$
Q_n^2(s,t)=\frac1n\sum_ah_a^1(s)h_a^1(t).
$$

For the leading mean-field theorem, replace it by

$$
Q^2(s,t)=\mathbb E_1[\phi(Z(s))\phi(Z(t))].
$$

The group-1 rowwise law of large numbers gives $Q_n^2\to Q^2$. Continuity of the Gaussian expectations and uniform integrability allow the replacement inside expectations. A leave-one-out covariance separates a singled-out row $a$ when a quantitative proof requires it.

The exact Gaussian integration-by-parts formula has the form below with covariance $Q_n^2$. After the stated deterministic-covariance replacement, applying (46A.3) to (46A.7) gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E_{W^2}
[(W^2_{pa})^2
\phi'(z_p^2(x))\phi'(z_p^2(x'))]
\\
&\quad=D_2
+\frac1n\sum_{s,t}h_a^1(s)h_a^1(t)C_2(s,t).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.9}
$$

After summing the top-group-only index $p$, (46A.7) becomes

$$
\frac{D_3D_2}{n}\sum_a
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\tag{46A.10}
$$

plus

$$
\frac{D_3}{n^2}\sum_a
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\sum_{s,t}h_a^1(s)h_a^1(t)C_2(s,t).
\tag{46A.11}
$$

### Peel group 2: the branch created by group-3 Stein differentiation

The equality pattern of $p,q$ must be split before rowwise independence is used.

For $p=q$, define only for the next two displays

$$
P_{uv}(Z)=
\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\phi(Z(u))\phi(Z(v)).
$$

Then (46A.3) gives

$$
\mathbb E_{W^2}[(W^2_{pa})^2P_{uv}(z_p^2)]
=
\mathbb E_2[P_{uv}(Z)]
+\frac1n\sum_{s,t}h_a^1(s)h_a^1(t)
\mathbb E_2[\partial_s\partial_tP_{uv}(Z)].
\tag{46A.12}
$$

After summing $p$, the diagonal branch of (46A.8) becomes

$$
\frac1{n^2}\sum_a
\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\sum_{u,v}C_3(u,v)\mathbb E_2[P_{uv}(Z)]
\tag{46A.13}
$$

plus

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac1{n^3}\sum_a
&\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\sum_{u,v,s,t}C_3(u,v)h_a^1(s)h_a^1(t)
\\
&\hspace{35mm}\times
\mathbb E_2[\partial_s\partial_tP_{uv}(Z)].
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.14}
$$

For $p\ne q$, the two rows of $W^2$ are conditionally independent. Set

$$
L_u(Z)=\phi'(Z(x))\phi(Z(u)),
\qquad
R_v(Z)=\phi'(Z(x'))\phi(Z(v)).
$$

Using the one-weight identity (46A.2) on each row,

$$
\mathbb E_{W^2}[W^2_{pa}L_u(z_p^2)]
=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_sh_a^1(s)
\mathbb E_2[\partial_sL_u(Z)],
\tag{46A.15}
$$

$$
\mathbb E_{W^2}[W^2_{qa}R_v(z_q^2)]
=\frac1{\sqrt n}\sum_th_a^1(t)
\mathbb E_2[\partial_tR_v(Z)].
\tag{46A.16}
$$

There are $n(n-1)$ ordered pairs $p\ne q$. Their total contribution is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{n-1}{n^3}\sum_a
&\phi'(z_a^1(x))\phi'(z_a^1(x'))
\sum_{u,v,s,t}C_3(u,v)h_a^1(s)h_a^1(t)
\\
&\times
\mathbb E_2[\partial_sL_u(Z)]
\mathbb E_2[\partial_tR_v(Z)].
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.17}
$$

The derivatives in these equations are completely explicit. For example,

$$
\partial_sL_u(Z)
=
\mathbf 1_{s=x}\phi''(Z(x))\phi(Z(u))
+\mathbf 1_{s=u}\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(u)),
\tag{46A.18}
$$

and the analogous formula for $R_v$ replaces $x$ by $x'$. To expand the remaining second derivative without hiding any product-rule terms, regard $P_{uv}$ as four factor occurrences with labels and derivative orders

$$
(x,1),\quad(x',1),\quad(u,0),\quad(v,0).
$$

If these four pairs are denoted locally by $(a_j,m_j)$, with $\phi^{(0)}=\phi$, then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_s\partial_tP_{uv}(Z)
&=\sum_{j=1}^4
\mathbf 1_{a_j=s}\mathbf 1_{a_j=t}
\phi^{(m_j+2)}(Z(a_j))
\prod_{k\ne j}\phi^{(m_k)}(Z(a_k))
\\
&\quad+
\sum_{j\ne k}
\mathbf 1_{a_j=s}\mathbf 1_{a_k=t}
\phi^{(m_j+1)}(Z(a_j))
\phi^{(m_k+1)}(Z(a_k))
\prod_{l\ne j,k}\phi^{(m_l)}(Z(a_l)).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.19}
$$

The first sum differentiates one factor occurrence twice; the second differentiates two distinct occurrences. Repeated sample labels, such as $u=x$, are handled correctly because the occurrences remain distinct in the product rule.

### Peel group 1

After the group-2 peel, every remaining random factor is a function of one group-1 row. There is no explicit $W^1$ factor in this observable because the kernel differentiates with respect to $z^1$, not with respect to $W^1$. Consequently group 1 is removed by the rowwise law of large numbers, not by another Stein differentiation.

Let $Z\sim N(0,Q^1)$, where $Q^1$ is the deterministic covariance induced by the two fixed inputs. Then, for every integrable function $G$ occurring above,

$$
\frac1n\sum_aG(z_a^1(x),z_a^1(x'))
\longrightarrow
\mathbb E_1[G(Z(x),Z(x'))].
\tag{46A.20}
$$

After the deterministic replacements of $Q_n^3$ and $Q_n^2$, applying (46A.20) to (46A.10)--(46A.17) gives the complete five-branch expression

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[B_n^1]
&=D_1D_2D_3
\\
&\quad+\frac{D_3}{n}\sum_{s,t}C_2(s,t)
\mathbb E_1[
\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\phi(Z(s))\phi(Z(t))]
\\
&\quad+\frac{D_1}{n}\sum_{u,v}C_3(u,v)
\mathbb E_2[P_{uv}(Z)]
\\
&\quad+\frac1{n^2}\sum_{u,v,s,t}C_3(u,v)
\mathbb E_2[\partial_s\partial_tP_{uv}(Z)]
\\
&\hspace{34mm}\times
\mathbb E_1[
\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\phi(Z(s))\phi(Z(t))]
\\
&\quad+\frac{n-1}{n^2}\sum_{u,v,s,t}C_3(u,v)
\mathbb E_2[\partial_sL_u(Z)]
\mathbb E_2[\partial_tR_v(Z)]
\\
&\hspace{34mm}\times
\mathbb E_1[
\phi'(Z(x))\phi'(Z(x'))\phi(Z(s))\phi(Z(t))]
+o(1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{46A.21}
$$

The $o(1)$ records the two deterministic-covariance replacements. Equation (46A.21) is therefore a complete structural expansion after that mean-field reduction, not an exact finite-width $1/n$ asymptotic expansion. In particular, fluctuations of $Q_n^2,Q_n^3$ can alter the displayed $1/n$ coefficient even though they cannot alter the leading limit.

The five displayed branches have orders

$$
1,\qquad n^{-1},\qquad n^{-1},\qquad n^{-2},\qquad n^{-1},
$$

respectively. Thus every branch containing at least one Stein attachment vanishes in the leading fixed-depth mean-field expectation, while none was discarded before its index multiplicity was counted. The final result is

$$
\boxed{
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[B_n^1(x,x')]
=D_1D_2D_3.
}
\tag{46A.22}
$$

This proves the expectation limit under the stated smoothness, moment, deterministic-forward-Gram, and uniform-integrability assumptions. Upgrading (46A.22) to convergence of $B_n^1$ itself requires the separate four-path second-moment peel described in Section 44.

### Lessons for the peeling lemma

This execution makes the following refinements necessary.

1. A peel is an exact conditional Gaussian elimination followed by a separate mean-field replacement; these are not the same step.
2. Only indices whose remaining occurrences all belong to the current top group may be summed out during that peel.
3. Lower-group factors created by Stein differentiation must remain as boundary data for the next peel.
4. Equality patterns such as $p=q$ and $p\ne q$ must be separated before independence and width counting are used.
5. Deterministic Gram replacement in an expression containing singled-out rows requires joint convergence or a leave-finitely-many-out argument, plus uniform integrability for expectations.
6. A leading-order theorem may replace empirical Grams by their limits. An exact $1/n$ expansion must retain Gram fluctuations and their correlations.
7. The final group need not contain an explicit Gaussian weight. In this example it is peeled by a rowwise Gaussian law of large numbers.
8. Expectation convergence and concentration of the observable are distinct proof obligations.

# Part V. Small reusable examples

## 47. Equality-pattern example

Let $Z_i$ be iid copies of $Z$. Then

$$
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j}F(Z_i)G(Z_j)
=
\frac{n(n-1)}{n^2}
\left(
\frac1{n(n-1)}\sum_{i\ne j}F(Z_i)G(Z_j)
\right)
+
\frac1{n^2}\sum_iF(Z_i)G(Z_i).
$$

The off-diagonal partition has two free row blocks and converges to

$$
\mathbb E[F(Z)]\mathbb E[G(Z)].
$$

The diagonal partition has one free row block and is lower by $n^{-1}$ under this normalization. If the observable instead contains $n^{-1}\sum_iF(Z_i)G(Z_i)$, the diagonal partition is leading and converges to the joint moment.

This is the basic equality-partition mechanism behind a peel.

## 48. One-weight Stein example

Let $(X,Z)$ be centered jointly Gaussian. Then

$$
\mathbb E[XF(Z)]
=
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)\mathbb E[F'(Z)].
$$

The explicit weight disappears, one derivative is added to the nonlinear function, and the covariance inserts its lower-layer factor.

## 49. Two-weight Wick–Stein example

For centered jointly Gaussian $(X,Y,Z)$,

$$
\mathbb E[XYF(Z)]
=
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)\mathbb E[F(Z)]
+
\operatorname{Cov}(X,Z)
\operatorname{Cov}(Y,Z)
\mathbb E[F''(Z)].
$$

The first term is the pseudo-independent Wick branch. The second is the Onsager branch. In a network, their relative size is determined only after the resulting index constraints and lower-layer activations are summed.

# Appendix A. Original NTK sample execution

This appendix preserves the mathematical content of the supplied LaTeX sample in Markdown. The document preamble and package declarations are omitted, but its claims, definitions, recursions, proof sketches, and conclusions are retained as a source record. The notation in this appendix is not silently corrected; Parts II–IV contain the maintained versions.

## A.1 Title and stated purpose

**Neural Tangent Kernel: A First Principles Derivation**  
Amir Joudaki, ETH Zurich

The sample described itself as a first-principles derivation of the Neural Tangent Kernel using the law of large numbers, central limit theorem, and Gaussian integration identities. It aimed to:

- establish mean-field variance recursions;
- analyze kernel shifts through parameter updates;
- derive the classical NTK recursion;
- analyze possible off-diagonal backward interactions;
- argue that these corrections are negligible at infinite width.

## A.2 Network architecture in the sample

The sample used an input layer, $L-1$ hidden layers, and a scalar output:

$$
h^0(x)=x\in\mathbb R^{d_0},
$$

$$
z^\ell(x)=W^{\ell-1}h^{\ell-1}(x)+b^{\ell-1},
$$

$$
h^\ell(x)=\phi(z^\ell(x)),
\qquad 1\le\ell<L,
$$

and

$$
z^L(x)=W^{L-1}h^{L-1}(x)+b^{L-1}.
$$

Weights and biases were initialized as

$$
W^\ell_{ij}\sim N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_w^2}{d_\ell}\right),
\qquad
b_i^\ell\sim N(0,\sigma_b^2).
$$

For the sample derivation, the “loss” was changed from the squared loss in the baseline notes to the scalar output

$$
\mathcal L=z^L(x').
$$

The stated motivation was to isolate the gradient structure and obtain a simple first-moment boundary.

## A.3 Forward kernel recursion in the sample

The preactivation kernel was defined by

$$
\Sigma^\ell(x,x')
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\langle z^\ell(x),z^\ell(x')\rangle.
$$

The proposed infinite-width recursion was

$$
\Sigma^0(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2\frac{x^\top x'}{d_0}+\sigma_b^2,
$$

$$
\Sigma^{\ell+1}(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2
\mathbb E[\phi(z)\phi(z')]
+\sigma_b^2,
$$

where $(z,z')$ is centered Gaussian with covariance

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\Sigma^\ell(x,x) & \Sigma^\ell(x,x')\\
\Sigma^\ell(x,x') & \Sigma^\ell(x',x')
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The derivative kernel was defined as

$$
\dot\Sigma^\ell(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2
\mathbb E[\phi'(z)\phi'(z')],
$$

with the Gaussian covariance inherited from the previous forward layer.

## A.4 Influence function in the sample

For a parameter block $W^{k-1}$, the sample defined the influence on layer $\ell$ by

$$
\dot\Theta_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\left\langle
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial W^{k-1}},
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial W^{k-1}}
\right\rangle_{W^{k-1}}.
$$

Using the derivative of $Wa$, it rewrote this schematically as

$$
\dot\Theta_{k,\ell}
=
\langle h^{k-1}(x),h^{k-1}(x')\rangle
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_i
\left\langle
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial z^k},
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z^k}
\right\rangle_{z^k}.
$$

## A.5 Backward Jacobian moments in the sample

With

$$
J_j^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
:=
\frac{\partial z^\ell(x)}{\partial z_j^k},
$$

the sample introduced

$$
M_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{j=1}^{d_k}
J_{j,i}^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
J_j^{(L\leftarrow k)}(x')
\right],
$$

$$
Q_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\mathbb E\left[
\left(
\sum_{j=1}^{d_k}
J_{j,i}^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
J_j^{(L\leftarrow k)}(x')
\right)^2
\right],
$$

and

$$
V_{k,\ell}=Q_{k,\ell}-M_{k,\ell}^2.
$$

## A.6 First-moment recursion proposed in the sample

The sample stated

$$
M_{k,\ell}
=
\dot\Sigma^k(x,x')M_{k+1,\ell},
$$

and therefore

$$
M_{k,\ell}
=
M_{\ell,\ell}
\prod_{i=k}^{\ell-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

Its proof expanded the Jacobian recursion

$$
\frac{\partial z_i^{\ell+1}}{\partial z_j^\ell}
=
W^\ell_{ij}\phi'(z_j^\ell)
$$

inside $M_{k,\ell}$, producing a term of the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{k,\ell}
&=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_{i,j,s,r}
\mathbb E\Bigg[
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial z_s^{k+1}}
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z_r^{k+1}}
\\
&\qquad\qquad\times
\phi'(z_j^k(x))
\phi'(z_j^k(x'))
W^k_{sj}W^k_{rj}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

The source proof then invoked independence of the backward Jacobians from $W^k$ and used

$$
\mathbb E[W^k_{sj}W^k_{rj}]
=
\delta_{sr}\frac{\sigma_w^2}{d_k}.
$$

It concluded with a derivative-kernel factor times the next backward moment.

The maintained document does not adopt this independence step without a Wick–Stein analysis, because upper activation derivatives can contain preactivations depending on $W^k$.

## A.7 Boundary conditions proposed in the sample

The sample stated

$$
M_{\ell,\ell}
=
\begin{cases}
0,&\ell<L,\\
1,&\ell=L.
\end{cases}
$$

The reasoning was that

$$
M_{\ell,\ell}
=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_i
\mathbb E\left[
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z_i^\ell}
\right],
$$

which equals one at the scalar output and was asserted to vanish at hidden layers by independence and symmetry.

The resulting proposed complete first moment was

$$
M_{k,\ell}
=
\begin{cases}
0,&\ell<L,\\
\displaystyle\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x'),&\ell=L.
\end{cases}
$$

## A.8 Second-moment statement in the sample

The sample proposed

$$
Q_{k,\ell}
=
\left(\dot\Sigma^k(x,x')\right)^2Q_{k+1,\ell}
+O(1/d_k),
$$

with boundary relation

$$
Q_{\ell,\ell}
=
Q_{\ell+1,\ell+1}
\dot\Sigma^{\ell+1}(x',x')
\frac{d_{\ell+1}}{d_\ell}.
$$

The proof was described as following from an analogous Wick expansion, with the leading pairing producing the squared derivative-kernel term. The source did not completely define or prove all normalization and boundary details.

## A.9 NTK expression in the sample

For a learning-rate scaling of the form $\eta_k\propto\eta_0/d_{k-1}$, the source proposed the expected layer contribution

$$
\mathbb E[\dot\Theta_{k,L}]
=
\eta_0
\Sigma^{k-1}(x,x')
\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

Summing parameter blocks gave

$$
\sum_{k=1}^L\mathbb E[\dot\Theta_{k,L}]
=
\eta_0
\sum_{k=1}^L
\Sigma^{k-1}(x,x')
\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

The NTK recursion was then stated as

$$
\Theta^L(x,x')
=
\Theta^{L-1}(x,x')
\dot\Sigma^{L-1}(x,x')
+\Sigma^{L-1}(x,x'),
$$

with base case

$$
\Theta^1(x,x')=\Sigma^0(x,x').
$$

## A.10 Signal propagation remark

For $x=x'$, the source wrote

$$
\dot\Sigma^\ell(x,x)
=
\sigma_w^2
\mathbb E[\phi'(Z)^2],
$$

and observed that the product of derivative kernels controls backward signal propagation. It identified the edge-of-chaos condition with

$$
\chi_1
:=
\sigma_w^2
\mathbb E[\phi'(\sqrt{q^\star}Z)^2]
\approx1.
$$

## A.11 Extended Stein identities in the sample

For jointly Gaussian $(X,Y,Z)$, the source recorded identities of the form

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)Y]
=
\sigma_{XY}\mathbb E[f'(X)],
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)f(Y)Z]
=
\sigma_{XZ}\mathbb E[f'(X)f(Y)]
+
\sigma_{YZ}\mathbb E[f(X)f'(Y)].
$$

These were intended to control forward–backward dependence missed by naive independence.

## A.12 Tentative diagonal/off-diagonal system

The source proposed a coupled system

$$
D_{k,\ell}
=
\dot\Sigma^{k+1}D_{k+1,\ell}
+
\frac{\sigma_w^4}{d_k}
(\mu_{\phi''}^{k+1})^2
C^k_{\phi,\phi}
S_{k+1,\ell},
$$

$$
S_{k,\ell}
=
2\sigma_w^4
C^{k+1}_{\phi'',\phi''}
(\mu_\phi^{k+1})^2
D_{k+1,\ell}
+
(\mu_\phi^{k+1})^2
(\mu_{\phi''}^{k+1})^2
S_{k+1,\ell}.
$$

Here $D$ was intended to represent diagonal backward contributions and $S$ off-diagonal corrections. The source claimed:

1. off-diagonal corrections are $O(1/d_k)$ or carry activation-mean factors;
2. centered activations make some off-diagonal terms vanish;
3. the classical NTK is recovered at leading order.

These equations remain archival and tentative because their normalization, state definitions, and boundary conditions were not fully specified.

## A.13 Conclusions stated in the sample

The sample concluded that:

1. infinite width produces clean law-of-large-numbers recursions;
2. only the last layer contributes to the chosen first moment under the linear-output surrogate loss;
3. variance contributions vanish at infinite width;
4. off-diagonal backward interactions are negligible;
5. the NTK emerges from parameter-gradient inner products.

It also emphasized that the NTK is independent of the learning rate as an architectural kernel, while the learning rate enters the training update

$$
\Delta f=-\eta\Theta\,\partial_f\mathcal L.
$$

Finally, it suggested extension to residual networks, normalization layers, and other architectures by modifying the forward and backward recursions.

## A.14 Current archival status

This source execution remains important because it supplied the motivating example for the peeling program. It should not be cited as a completed proof without repairing:

- the inconsistent layer indexing;
- the change of loss;
- raw versus effective weight normalization;
- the dependence of upper backward Jacobians on the current weight layer;
- the definitions and normalization of the second-moment states;
- the tentative off-diagonal recursion.

The checked backward-kernel execution in Part IV is the current replacement for the corresponding independence-based step.
