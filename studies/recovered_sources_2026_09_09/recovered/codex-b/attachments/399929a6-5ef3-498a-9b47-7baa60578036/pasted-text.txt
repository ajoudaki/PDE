The clean version is
\\[
\boxed{
\text{actual GD}\_{n,\eta\_n}
\approx
\text{gradient flow}\_n
\approx
\text{Euler}\_{n,\delta}
\approx
\text{oracle}\_{n,\delta}
\longrightarrow
\text{Euler}\_{\infty,\delta}
\approx
\text{gradient flow}\_\infty .

The objects differ as follows:
Object	Width	Time	Meaning
Actual GD	finite \\(n\\)	discrete, step \\(\eta\_n\\)	The real neural-network training algorithm
Finite-width gradient flow	finite \\(n\\)	continuous	The exact ODE obtained from the finite network’s gradients
Finite-width Euler	finite \\(n\\)	discrete, step \\(\delta\\)	A proof-only numerical approximation of that ODE
Finite-width oracle	finite \\(n\\)	discrete, step \\(\delta\\)	Euler where empirical averages are replaced by their predicted population values
Population Euler	infinite width	discrete, step \\(\delta\\)	The limiting population update at fixed \\(\delta\\)
Continuum flow	infinite width	continuous	The final autonomous mean-field/gradient-flow system


The distinction between gradient flow and Euler is easiest to see for an ordinary scalar function. Suppose the continuous dynamics are
\\[

\\[

\=
x\_k^{\delta}
\-
\delta g(x\_k^{\delta}).
\\]Euler uses the slope at the beginning of each step and follows that constant slope for time \\(\delta\\). The true flow allows the slope to change continuously throughout the interval. Under suitable smoothness,
\\[
\sup\_{t\le T}|x^\delta(t)-x(t)|
\le C\_T\delta.
\\]The neural-network distinction is identical. Let “state” mean all current values of
\\[

\\[

\delta

\\]So gradient flow and finite-width Euler use the same finite-width vector field \\(V\_n\\). The difference is only continuous versus discretized time.

\\[

\+
\eta\_n

\- \\(\eta\_n=n^{-2}\\) is fixed by the theorem and is part of the actual training algorithm.

More precisely, fix a step \(m\). Then
\[ (p_1,\overline X_{1,m}),\ldots,(p_n,\overline X_{n,m}) \]
are independent copies of the population pair
\[ (P,X_m^{\mathrm{pop}}). \]
But for one fixed particle \(i\),
\[ (p_i,\overline X_{i,0}), \quad (p_i,\overline X_{i,1}), \quad (p_i,\overline X_{i,2}),\ldots \]
are certainly not independent across time. They form one evolving trajectory with the same permanent label \(p_i\).
The reason for independence across particles is simple. Once the population system has produced the deterministic numbers
\[ r_0,r_1,\ldots,r_{m-1}, \]
the oracle state at step \(m\) is a deterministic function of that particle’s own initial pair:
\[ \overline X_{i,m} = X_{i,0} - \delta p_i \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}r_\ell. \]
Likewise, the population variable satisfies
\[ X_m^{\mathrm{pop}} = X_0 - \delta P \sum_{\ell=0}^{m-1}r_\ell. \]
Thus
\[ (p_i,\overline X_{i,m}) = \left( p_i,\, X_{i,0}-\delta p_i\sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \right), \]
while
\[ (P,X_m^{\mathrm{pop}}) = \left( P,\, X_0-\delta P\sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \right). \]
The initial pairs
\[ (p_i,X_{i,0}) \]
are independent copies of \((P,X_0)\). Applying the same deterministic formula separately to each pair preserves that independence. Therefore the resulting oracle pairs are independent copies of the population pair.
An even stronger statement is true: the complete oracle trajectories
\[ (p_i,\overline X_{i,0},\ldots,\overline X_{i,m}) \]
are independent across \(i\) and have the same distribution as
\[ (P,X_0^{\mathrm{pop}},\ldots,X_m^{\mathrm{pop}}). \]
They are independent across particles, not across time.
If you want to phrase this in Markov language, include the fixed label in the state:
\[ P_{m+1}=P_m, \qquad X_{m+1}^{\mathrm{pop}} = X_m^{\mathrm{pop}}-\delta P_mr_m. \]
A variable remaining constant does not violate the Markov property. The next pair is completely determined by the current pair. But we do not actually need a Markov argument here; the required fact is simply independence across particle index \(i\).

The condition
\[ \frac{\log(1/\delta_n)}{n}\longrightarrow 0 \]
is needed only if we choose a single simultaneous schedule \(\delta=\delta_n\to0\) while \(n\to\infty\).
It means
\[ \delta_n=e^{-o(n)}: \]
the Euler step may shrink, but only subexponentially in \(n\). Examples that work are
\[ \delta_n=\frac1n,\qquad \delta_n=\frac1{n^{10}},\qquad \delta_n=e^{-\sqrt n}. \]
Saying merely
\[ \delta_n\gg e^{-n} \]
is not quite sufficient. For example,
\[ \delta_n=e^{-n/2} \]
is much larger than \(e^{-n}\), but
\[ \frac{\log(1/\delta_n)}n=\frac12, \]
which does not approach zero. The precise informal statement is:
\[ \boxed{\delta_n\text{ must be much larger than }e^{-cn} \text{ for every fixed }c>0.} \]
But the original proof does not assume any relation between \(\delta\) and \(n\). It uses the iterated order
\[ \boxed{\text{fix }\delta;\qquad n\to\infty;\qquad\delta\to0.} \]
For fixed \(\delta\), the number of Euler steps \(M\approx T/\delta\) is fixed, so the logarithm is just a fixed constant.
The condition above is therefore only a sufficient condition if we voluntarily replace the iterated limit by a simultaneous schedule.

The next step: finite oracle to population Euler
We have already compared
\[ \text{finite Euler}\approx\text{finite oracle}. \]
Now we prove
\[ \boxed{\text{finite oracle}\longrightarrow\text{population Euler}.} \]
The key point is that this is not another dynamical approximation. The oracle particles follow the population dynamics exactly. The only remaining approximation is that \(n\) samples are being used to represent a probability distribution.
1. The population Euler process
Let \((P,Z_0)\) be the random initial state of one representative population particle.
At step \(m\), define the population residual
\[ r_m=\mathbb E\!\left[P\phi(Z_m)\right]-y. \]
The transformed Euler update, written back in terms of \(Z\), is
\[ Z_{m+1} = F^{-1}\!\left(F(Z_m)-\delta P r_m\right), \]
where
\[ F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3}. \]
To shorten the next argument, call this deterministic update rule
\[ G_m(p,z) = F^{-1}\!\left(F(z)-\delta p r_m\right). \]
Then the population update is simply
\[ Z_{m+1}=G_m(P,Z_m). \]
The number \(r_m\) is deterministic: it is calculated from the complete population distribution at step \(m\).
2. The oracle particles
Initially, sample independently
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,0}), \qquad i=1,\ldots,n, \]
from the distribution of \((P,Z_0)\).
The oracle then uses the same population residual \(r_m\) for every particle:
\[ \bar z_{i,m+1} = G_m(p_i,\bar z_{i,m}). \]
The permanent label \(p_i\) is not resampled. It remains attached to particle \(i\).
3. Why the oracle particles remain independent population samples
Initially,
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,0}) \]
are independent copies of
\[ (P,Z_0). \]
Suppose that at step \(m\),
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,m}) \]
are independent copies of
\[ (P,Z_m). \]
Every oracle particle is then updated by the same deterministic rule:
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,m}) \longmapsto \bigl(p_i,G_m(p_i,\bar z_{i,m})\bigr). \]
Because this rule acts separately on each particle, it cannot create dependence between different particles.
Moreover, because every particle receives the same rule, their distributions remain identical. Finally,
\[ \bigl(P,G_m(P,Z_m)\bigr) = (P,Z_{m+1}). \]
Therefore,
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,m+1}) \]
are independent copies of
\[ (P,Z_{m+1}). \]
Repeating this argument proves that this is true at every step.
This does not say that one particle is independent across time. In fact,
\[ \bar z_{i,0},\bar z_{i,1},\ldots \]
are strongly dependent: later states are determined by earlier states. Independence is across particles:
\[ \text{trajectory of particle }1,\ldots, \text{trajectory of particle }n \]
are independent copies of the population trajectory.
4. What convergence to the population means
Take any bounded quantity \(\psi(p,z)\) that we want to measure. Because the oracle pairs at step \(m\) are independent population samples, the law of large numbers gives
\[ \boxed{ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \psi(p_i,\bar z_{i,m}) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\!\left[\psi(P,Z_m)\right]. } \]
This is the precise meaning of
\[ \text{oracle empirical population} \longrightarrow \text{population law}. \]
For example, choose
\[ \psi(p,z)=p\phi(z). \]
Then
\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\phi(\bar z_{i,m}) \longrightarrow \mathbb E[P\phi(Z_m)]. \]
Consequently, the oracle’s empirical residual converges to the population residual:
\[ \boxed{\bar r_{n,m}\longrightarrow r_m.} \]
5. The statement is also true for entire trajectories
For fixed \(\delta\), there are only finitely many steps
\[ m=0,\ldots,M, \qquad M=\left\lfloor\frac T\delta\right\rfloor. \]
The complete oracle trajectories
\[ (p_i,\bar z_{i,0},\ldots,\bar z_{i,M}) \]
are independent copies of the complete population trajectory
\[ (P,Z_0,\ldots,Z_M). \]
Therefore, for any bounded trajectory-level quantity \(\Psi\),
\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \Psi(p_i,\bar z_{i,0},\ldots,\bar z_{i,M}) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\!\left[ \Psi(P,Z_0,\ldots,Z_M) \right]. \]
So we retain all correlations across time. We never claim that the states at different steps are independent.
6. Combining the two comparisons
Let \(z_{i,m}\) denote the actual finite-Euler particle and \(\bar z_{i,m}\) its oracle copy. Suppose \(\psi\) changes by at most \(L\) times the change in \(z\). Then
\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,z_{i,m}) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z_m)] \right| \\ &\le L\max_i|z_{i,m}-\bar z_{i,m}| \\ &\quad+ \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,\bar z_{i,m}) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z_m)] \right|. \end{aligned} \]
The first term vanishes because finite Euler is close to the oracle.
The second term vanishes because the oracle particles are independent population samples.
Hence
\[ \boxed{ \text{finite Euler} \longrightarrow \text{population Euler} } \]
at every fixed \(\delta\).
The only remaining arrow is
\[ \boxed{ \text{population Euler at step }\delta \approx \text{population continuous flow}, } \]
which is the infinite-population version of the ordinary Euler-to-flow argument.

\[ r_m=\mathbb E[P\phi(Z_m)]-y. \]

\[ F(\bar z_{i,m+1}) = F(\bar z_{i,m})-\delta p_i r_m. \]

Nothing in row \(i\)’s update uses the values in another row. It only uses:
1. its own permanent label \(p_i\);
2. its own current state;
3. the deterministic population number \(r_m\).
Therefore independent rows remain independent.
At the next step, the population system computes a new exact number \(r_{m+1}\), writes that on the board, and all rows update again. There is no resampling. Each row remembers its past, so values within one row are dependent across time. But different rows remain independent copies of the same complete population evolution.
A compact way to see this is
\[ \bar z_{i,m} = \text{a deterministic function of } (p_i,z_{i,0},r_0,\ldots,r_{m-1}). \]

\[ \boxed{ \text{population system} = \text{the distribution of one representative row}, } \]
while
\[ \boxed{ \text{oracle} = n\text{ independent rows sampled from that distribution}. } \]
The only qualification to “any fancy quantity with an \(i\)” is that it should be a per-particle quantity with a well-defined finite average. Quantities coupling multiple particles—such as the maximum over all particles—require separate arguments.
This also makes the difference from actual finite Euler tangible. In actual finite Euler, the number on the board is

It is computed from the same finite rows. Hence every row affects the public number, which then affects every other row. That couples the particles.

This is the complete result for the toy model.
Let the initial particle pairs
\[ (p_i,z_{i,0}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n, \]
be independent copies of a random pair \((P,Z_0)\), where \(P\in\{-1,1\}\). The label \(p_i\) never changes; only \(z_{i,k}\) is trained.
Define
\[ \phi(z)=\arctan z, \qquad f_{n,k}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\phi(z_{i,k}), \qquad r_{n,k}=f_{n,k}-y. \]
Exact finite-width GD is
\[ z_{i,k+1} = z_{i,k} - \eta_n p_i r_{n,k}\phi'(z_{i,k}). \]
Represent it in continuous training time \(t=k\eta_n\), holding each iterate constant until the next update. Linear interpolation gives the same limit.
Assume only
\[ \eta_n\longrightarrow0. \]
The limiting population state is the probability distribution
\[ \mu_t=\operatorname{Law}(P,X(t)), \]
where
\[ F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3}, \qquad X(0)=F(Z_0), \qquad Z(t)=F^{-1}(X(t)). \]
Given the current population law \(\mu_t\), compute its residual
\[ r(\mu_t) = \mathbb E[P\phi(Z(t))]-y, \]
and evolve each population particle by
\[ \frac{dX(t)}{dt} = -P\,r(\mu_t). \]
This is autonomous: the current law \(\mu_t\) determines the current residual, which determines the next movement. No initialization record, external fitted trajectory, or past history is required once the current law is known.
For every fixed \(T\) and every fixed bounded function \(\psi(p,z)\) that is Lipschitz in \(z\),
\[ \boxed{ \sup_{0\le t\le T} \left| \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \psi(p_i,z_i^{\mathrm{GD}}(t)) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z(t))] \right| \longrightarrow0 \quad\text{in probability}. } \]
This is uniform in time for each fixed \(\psi\). In particular, it applies to the prediction by choosing
\[ \psi(p,z)=p\phi(z), \]
and therefore also to the residual and normalized loss.

1. Why the nonlinear coordinate is introduced
Let
\[ q(z)=\phi'(z)=\frac1{1+z^2}, \qquad X=F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3}. \]
Since
\[ F'(z)=1+z^2=\frac1{q(z)}, \]
the raw continuous gradient flow
\[ \dot z_i=-p_i r_nq(z_i) \]
becomes exactly
\[ \dot X_i=-p_i r_n. \]
Thus continuous flow transforms exactly.
Discrete GD does not. If
\[ v_i=-p_i r_n, \]
then one GD step is
\[ z_i^+=z_i+\eta_nq(z_i)v_i. \]
Because \(F\) is cubic,
\[ \begin{aligned} F(z_i^+) ={}&F(z_i)+\eta_n v_i \\ &+\eta_n^2z_iq(z_i)^2v_i^2 +\frac{\eta_n^3}{3}q(z_i)^3v_i^3. \end{aligned} \]
The first two terms are transformed Euler. The remaining terms are the nonlinear-coordinate defect.
Set
\[ R=\frac{\pi}{2}+|y|. \]
Since \(|r_n|\le R\), the one-step coordinate defect is at most
\[ \eta_n^2 \left(R^2+\frac{R^3}{3}\right). \]
The exact continuous flow also differs from one ideal Euler step by at most
\[ \frac R2\eta_n^2. \]
If \(E_k\) is the largest transformed-coordinate difference between GD and the finite flow at time \(k\eta_n\), then
\[ E_{k+1} \le (1+\eta_n)E_k + \eta_n^2 \left( R^2+\frac{R^3}{3}+\frac R2 \right). \]
Because \(E_0=0\), discrete Gronwall gives
\[ \max_{k\eta_n\le T}E_k \le C_1(T,y)\eta_n, \]
where \(C_1(T,y)\) does not depend on \(n\).
Therefore
\[ \text{raw finite GD} \approx \text{finite transformed flow} \]
whenever \(\eta_n\to0\).

2. Replace the finite flow by a coarse Euler scheme
Choose an auxiliary proof mesh \(\delta>0\), unrelated to \(\eta_n\).
Finite transformed Euler is
\[ X^n_{i,m+1} = X^n_{i,m}-\delta p_i r^n_m, \]
where
\[ r^n_m = \frac1n\sum_j p_j\phi\!\left(F^{-1}(X^n_{j,m})\right)-y. \]
The transformed vector field is bounded by \(R\) and is 1-Lipschitz in the largest coordinate difference. Consequently, ordinary Euler analysis gives
\[ \sup_{t\le T} \max_i |X_i^{n,\mathrm{flow}}(t)-X_i^{n,\mathrm{Euler}}(t)| \le C_2(T,y)\delta, \]
uniformly in \(n\).
Thus the actual microscopic GD trajectory is within
\[ C_1(T,y)\eta_n+C_2(T,y)\delta \]
of the coarse finite Euler trajectory.
The purpose of \(\delta\) is that the number of coarse updates
\[ M=\left\lceil\frac T\delta\right\rceil \]
is fixed while \(n\to\infty\).

3. Introduce population Euler and the oracle
Population Euler evolves one representative population particle:
\[ r_m = \mathbb E\!\left[ P\phi(F^{-1}(X_m)) \right]-y, \]\[ X_{m+1}=X_m-\delta P r_m. \]
The finite oracle starts from the same finite initialization as finite Euler, but uses the population residual:
\[ \bar X_{i,m+1} = \bar X_{i,m}-\delta p_i r_m. \]
The oracle also has an empirical residual,
\[ \bar r^n_m = \frac1n\sum_i p_i\phi(F^{-1}(\bar X_{i,m}))-y, \]
but it does not use this number in its update.
Let
\[ E_m = \max_i|X^n_{i,m}-\bar X_{i,m}|. \]
Subtracting the finite-Euler and oracle updates gives
\[ E_{m+1} \le E_m+\delta|r^n_m-r_m|. \]
Insert \(\bar r^n_m\):
\[ |r^n_m-r_m| \le |r^n_m-\bar r^n_m| + |\bar r^n_m-r_m|. \]
Because \(\phi\circ F^{-1}\) is 1-Lipschitz,
\[ |r^n_m-\bar r^n_m|\le E_m. \]
Hence
\[ E_{m+1} \le (1+\delta)E_m + \delta|\bar r^n_m-r_m|. \]
Since \(E_0=0\),
\[ \max_{m\le M}E_m \le (e^T-1) \max_{m\le M}|\bar r^n_m-r_m|. \]
The finite/oracle difference is therefore controlled entirely by the oracle’s sampling error in the residual.

4. Oracle sampling converges to population Euler
The population residuals \(r_0,\ldots,r_{M-1}\) are deterministic once the initial population law is fixed.
Each complete oracle trajectory is therefore a deterministic function of its own initialization:
\[ (p_i,\bar X_{i,0}) \longmapsto (p_i,\bar X_{i,0},\ldots,\bar X_{i,M}). \]
Because the initial pairs are independent copies, the complete oracle trajectories are independent copies of the population Euler trajectory.
In particular, at every step \(m\),
\[ p_i\phi(F^{-1}(\bar X_{i,m})) \]
are independent copies of
\[ P\phi(F^{-1}(X_m)). \]
Hoeffding’s inequality and a union bound give, with probability at least \(1-\alpha/2\),
\[ \max_{m\le M}|\bar r^n_m-r_m| \le \pi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }. \]
Consequently,
\[ \max_{m\le M}E_m \le (e^T-1)\pi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }. \]
There is also a second, distinct sampling error. Let \(\psi\) be the observable in the headline result, and let
\[ B_\psi=\sup\psi-\inf\psi \]
be the length of its range. The same argument gives, with probability at least \(1-\alpha/2\),
\[ \max_{m\le M} \left| \frac1n\sum_i \psi(p_i,\bar Z_{i,m}) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z_m)] \right| \le B_\psi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }, \]
where
\[ \bar Z_{i,m}=F^{-1}(\bar X_{i,m}), \qquad Z_m=F^{-1}(X_m). \]
The first sampling error controls how inaccurate residual feedback affects the finite dynamics. The second directly controls the empirical average of the chosen observable \(\psi\).
For complete trajectories, define
\[ \Gamma_i = (p_i,\bar Z_{i,0},\ldots,\bar Z_{i,M}). \]
These are independent copies of
\[ \Gamma=(P,Z_0,\ldots,Z_M). \]
Thus, for every integrable trajectory observable \(\Psi\),
\[ \frac1n\sum_i\Psi(\Gamma_i) \longrightarrow \mathbb E[\Psi(\Gamma)]. \]
No independence across time is asserted or needed; independence is across complete particle trajectories.

5. Remove the population Euler mesh
The population flow is
\[ \dot X(t)=-P r(t), \qquad r(t)=\mathbb E[P\phi(F^{-1}(X(t)))]-y. \]
To prove existence and uniqueness concretely, write
\[ X(t)=X_0-Ps(t). \]
Then the scalar accumulated residual satisfies
\[ \frac{ds(t)}{dt} = \mathbb E\!\left[ P\phi(F^{-1}(X_0-Ps(t))) \right]-y. \]
The right-hand side is bounded by \(R\) and is 1-Lipschitz in \(s\). Therefore this scalar ODE has a unique global solution. This proves that the population flow is well-defined.
Population Euler is ordinary Euler for the same scalar equation. If \(s_m\) is its discrete accumulated residual, then
\[ \max_{m\delta\le T}|s_m-s(m\delta)| \le \frac R2(e^T-1)\delta. \]
Because
\[ X_m=X_0-Ps_m, \qquad X(t)=X_0-Ps(t), \]
the same estimate holds for \(X_m-X(m\delta)\). Holding Euler constant between grid points adds at most \(R\delta\). Therefore
\[ \sup_{t\le T}|X^{\mathrm{population\ Euler}}(t)-X(t)| \le C_2(T,y)\delta. \]
No oracle is needed here: population Euler and population flow already live on the same population state and use the same vector field.

6. Add the five errors
At any time \(t\), insert all intermediate systems between the finite-GD average and the population-flow expectation:
\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,z_i^{\mathrm{GD}}(t)) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z(t))] \right| \\ &\le \underbrace{ |\text{finite GD average}-\text{finite flow average}| }_{\text{actual-step error}} \\ &\quad+ \underbrace{ |\text{finite flow average}-\text{finite Euler average}| }_{\text{finite Euler-mesh error}} \\ &\quad+ \underbrace{ |\text{finite Euler average}-\text{oracle average}| }_{\text{feedback-coupling error}} \\ &\quad+ \underbrace{ |\text{oracle average}-\text{population Euler expectation}| }_{\text{direct sampling error for }\psi} \\ &\quad+ \underbrace{ |\text{population Euler expectation}-\text{population flow expectation}| }_{\text{population Euler-mesh error}}. \end{aligned} \]
Suppose \(\psi\) is \(L_\psi\)-Lipschitz in \(z\). Since \(F^{-1}\) is 1-Lipschitz, the five terms are bounded respectively by
\[ L_\psi C_1(T,y)\eta_n, \]\[ L_\psi C_2(T,y)\delta, \]\[ L_\psi(e^T-1)\pi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }, \]\[ B_\psi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }, \]

\[ L_\psi C_2(T,y)\delta. \]

\[ \begin{aligned} \sup_{t\le T} \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,z_i^{\mathrm{GD}}(t)) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z(t))] \right| \le{}& L_\psi C_1(T,y)\eta_n \\ &+2L_\psi C_2(T,y)\delta \\ &+ \left[L_\psi(e^T-1)\pi+B_\psi\right] \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }. \end{aligned} \]

The right-hand side is bounded and 1-Lipschitz in \(s\), so this scalar ODE has a unique global solution. Therefore the pop flow is well-defined and autonomous.
Pop Euler is ordinary Euler for this same equation. Its error satisfies
\[ \max_{m\delta\le T}|s_m-s(m\delta)| \le \frac R2(e^T-1)\delta, \qquad R=\frac\pi2+|y|. \]
Because
\[ X_m=X_0-Ps_m, \qquad X(t)=X_0-Ps(t), \]
the same estimate controls \(X_m-X(m\delta)\). Including the movement between grid points gives
\[ \sup_{t\le T} |X^{\mathrm{pop\ Euler}}(t)-X^{\mathrm{pop\ flow}}(t)| \le C_T\delta. \]

For any time \(t\),
\[ \begin{aligned} & \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,z_i^{\mathrm{GD}}(t)) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z(t))] \right| \\ &\le |\text{finite GD avg}-\text{finite flow avg}| &&\text{(actual step)} \\ &\quad+ |\text{finite flow avg}-\text{finite Euler avg}| &&\text{(finite mesh)} \\ &\quad+ |\text{finite Euler avg}-\text{oracle avg}| &&\text{(feedback)} \\ &\quad+ |\text{oracle avg}-\text{pop Euler avg}| &&\text{(sampling of }\psi\text{)} \\ &\quad+ |\text{pop Euler avg}-\text{pop flow avg}| &&\text{(pop mesh)}. \end{aligned} \]
If \(\psi\) is \(L_\psi\)-Lipschitz in \(z\), the five terms are bounded by
\[ L_\psi C_T \left( \eta_n+\eta_n\sqrt n+\eta_n^2n \right), \]\[ L_\psi C_T\delta, \]\[ L_\psi(e^T-1)\pi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }, \]\[ B_\psi \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }, \]
and
\[ L_\psi C_T\delta. \]
Therefore, with probability at least \(1-\alpha\),
\[ \begin{aligned} & \sup_{t\le T} \left| \frac1n\sum_i\psi(p_i,z_i^{\mathrm{GD}}(t)) - \mathbb E[\psi(P,Z(t))] \right| \\ &\le L_\psi C_T \left( \eta_n+\eta_n\sqrt n+\eta_n^2n \right) \\ &\quad+ 2L_\psi C_T\delta \\ &\quad+ \left[ L_\psi(e^T-1)\pi+B_\psi \right] \sqrt{ \frac{\log(4(M+1)/\alpha)}{2n} }. \end{aligned} \]
Now:
1. Choose \(\delta\) small enough to control both mesh errors.
2. Keep \(\delta\) fixed and take \(n\to\infty\).
3. The learning-rate term vanishes because \[ \eta_n\sqrt n\to0. \]
4. The two sampling terms vanish because \(M\) is fixed.
5. Finally let \(\delta\to0\).
This proves uniform compact-time convergence in probability.
The theorem in the audited document fixes
\[ \eta_n=n^{-2}, \]
which satisfies
\[ \eta_n\sqrt n=n^{-3/2}\to0. \]
More generally, the displayed proof estimate closes for every learning-rate sequence satisfying
\[ \eta_n=o(n^{-1/2}). \]

Yes—mostly. The cubic
\[ F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3} \]
is not the fundamental idea. It appears because the proof deliberately asks for a coordinate satisfying
\[ F'(z)\phi'(z)=1. \]
For \(\phi(z)=\arctan z\),
\[ \phi'(z)=\frac1{1+z^2}, \]
so
\[ F'(z)=\frac1{\phi'(z)}=1+z^2, \]
whose integral happens to be cubic. The polynomial identity is convenient, but the structural idea is simply:
\[ F(z)=\int_0^z\frac{du}{\phi'(u)}. \]
Arctan was selected because this construction has unusually clean global properties.
What properties of \(\phi\) are actually used?
Write
\[ q(z)=\phi'(z). \]
There is not one single minimal assumption: different parts of the theorem use different properties.
Property	Why the proof uses it
\(q(z)\neq0\), with constant sign	Makes \(F'(z)=1/q(z)\) meaningful and \(F\) invertible
\(F(\mathbb R)=\mathbb R\)	Makes the transformed coordinate global rather than valid only locally
\(q\) bounded	Makes \(F^{-1}\) and \(\phi\circ F^{-1}\) Lipschitz
\(q\) Lipschitz	Gives width-independent stability and controls the discrete coordinate-change defect
\(\phi\) bounded	Controls outputs, residuals, \(C\), \(W^{(2)}\), and prevents finite-time blow-up in the present proof
\(1/q\) has at most polynomial growth	Makes \(F\) admissible in the fixed tensor-program theorem

Let us make each statement precise.
1. The transformation must exist globally
The most direct condition is that \(q\) never vanish. Assuming \(q>0\),
\[ F(z)=\int_0^z\frac{du}{q(u)} \]
is strictly increasing.
To obtain a map from all of \(\mathbb R\) onto all of \(\mathbb R\), we need
\[ \int_0^\infty\frac{du}{q(u)}=\infty, \qquad \int_{-\infty}^0\frac{du}{q(u)}=\infty \]
in absolute magnitude.
A positive bounded \(q\) automatically gives this divergence. No positive lower bound on \(q\) is needed: \(q(z)\) may approach zero at infinity, as it does for arctan.
If \(q\) vanishes, as for ReLU on half of the line, this global transformation breaks.
2. The transformed forward maps must be stable
Let
\[ J=F^{-1}, \qquad h(x)=\phi(J(x)). \]
Differentiating gives
\[ J'(x)=q(J(x)), \]
and
\[ h'(x)=q(J(x))^2. \]
Therefore, if \(q\) is bounded, then both \(J\) and \(h\) are globally Lipschitz.
The transformed state \(X\) can then be converted back into the raw preactivation and activation without magnifying small errors:
\[ |J(X)-J(\widetilde X)| \le C|X-\widetilde X|, \]\[ |h(X)-h(\widetilde X)| \le C|X-\widetilde X|. \]
The proof also needs \(q\) itself to be Lipschitz because quantities such as
\[ D=C\,q(S) \]
must vary stably when \(S\) changes. A convenient sufficient condition is
\[ \sup_z|q(z)|<\infty, \qquad \sup_z|q'(z)|<\infty. \]
Equivalently, \(\phi'\) and \(\phi''\) are bounded.
3. What is really needed for raw GD versus transformed Euler?
The cubic identity is stronger than necessary. The direct property needed is the following uniform second-order approximation:
\[ \sup_{\substack{z\in\mathbb R\\|u|\le u_0}} \frac{ \left| F\!\left(z+q(z)u\right)-F(z)-u \right| }{u^2} <\infty. \]
Its meaning is simple:
* raw GD changes \(z\) by \(q(z)u\);
* after transforming, the first-order change should be \(u\);
* everything left over must be at most order \(u^2\).
For arctan, this remainder can be written exactly because \(F\) is cubic. Exact algebra is not required.
If \(q>0\) and \(q'\) is bounded, the displayed second-order property holds for sufficiently small \(u\). Therefore a broad activation class—not just arctan—has the required GD-to-flow comparison.
4. Moderate growth is genuinely relevant to this particular proof
The tensor-program theorem used in the audited proof accepts coordinate maps with polynomial growth.
Thus the proof needs maps such as
\[ F,\qquad F^{-1},\qquad \phi\circ F^{-1},\qquad q \]
to have suitable polynomial growth.
A convenient sufficient condition is
\[ \frac1{q(z)} \le C(1+|z|^k) \]
for some fixed \(k\).
Arctan is ideal because
\[ \frac1{q(z)}=1+z^2. \]
This is one place where arctan’s algebra is not entirely cosmetic.
For example, \(\tanh\) has
\[ q(z)=\operatorname{sech}^2z, \qquad \frac1{q(z)}=\cosh^2z, \]
so the natural \(F\) grows exponentially. The dynamical argument may still be possible, but the fixed polynomial-growth tensor-program theorem would no longer apply directly.
Thus:
* the cubic formula is incidental;
* polynomially controlled reciprocal derivative is proof-technologically important.
5. Bounded activation is used for global control
The proof repeatedly uses
\[ |\phi(z)|\le C. \]
This bounds the hidden activations, network output, residual, readout velocity, and operator velocity. It supplies global-in-time bounds on every compact interval.
Boundedness is not mathematically necessary for every possible proof. An unbounded activation might be handled using stronger moment or energy estimates. But boundedness is an important assumption of this proof architecture.
6. The strict nonlinearity conclusion needs separate nondegeneracy
The existence and convergence proof does not by itself prove meaningful feature learning. For that, the final section needs conditions such as
\[ \mathbb E[\phi(Z)^2]>0, \]\[ \mathbb E[(\phi(Z)\phi'(Z))^2]>0, \]
under the relevant initial Gaussian laws.
These prevent the activation and initial backpropagated signal from vanishing.
It also needs \(\phi\) not to be affine on the support of the initial law. Since the Gaussian law has full support, continuity and global non-affinity imply a positive affine-regression residual.
Oddness and the exact value \(\pi/2\) are not fundamental. A bounded, smooth, monotone, non-affine activation with the other properties could play the same role.

Why transform instead of working directly in \(z\)?
In raw coordinates, the first-layer dynamics are
\[ \dot z=q(z)P, \]
where \(P\) is the backpropagated field.
To compare two states, one must control
\[ q(z)P-q(\widetilde z)\widetilde P. \]
Splitting the difference gives
\[ \begin{aligned} \|q(z)P-q(\widetilde z)\widetilde P\|_2 \le{}& \|q(z)(P-\widetilde P)\|_2 \\ &+ \|(q(z)-q(\widetilde z))\widetilde P\|_2. \end{aligned} \]
The first term is harmless because \(q\) is bounded. The second is the obstruction.
Knowing only
\[ \|z-\widetilde z\|_2\quad\text{and}\quad\|\widetilde P\|_2 \]
does not control the product. Multiplication is not a Lipschitz map from \(L^2\times L^2\) into \(L^2\).
A finite-dimensional example shows the problem. Suppose \(\widetilde P\) is zero except at one coordinate, where it equals \(\sqrt n\). Then its normalized norm is only
\[ \|\widetilde P\|_n=1. \]
Change \(z\) by order one only at that same coordinate. The normalized \(z\)-difference is
\[ \|z-\widetilde z\|_n=\frac1{\sqrt n}\to0, \]
but multiplying by \(\widetilde P\) produces an order-one normalized error. Therefore the raw vector field is not uniformly Lipschitz in width.
The transformation removes precisely this product:
\[ \dot z=q(z)P, \qquad F'(z)=\frac1{q(z)} \]
implies
\[ \dot X=F'(z)\dot z=P. \]
All dependence on \(z\) is moved into the forward map
\[ \phi(z)=h(X), \]
and \(h\) is globally Lipschitz. The dangerous multiplication of an \(L^2\) state error by an unbounded \(L^2\) backpropagated field disappears.
This is the design philosophy:
\[ \text{flatten the coordinate-dependent gate }q(z) \text{ before proving width-uniform stability.} \]
Up to an affine rescaling, \(F'(z)=1/q(z)\) is essentially the unique coordinate change that cancels the gate exactly.
Was the transformation strictly necessary?
For the toy particle model: no.
There, \(p_i\in\{-1,1\}\) is uniformly bounded, so the raw vector field
\[ -p_i r_nq(z_i) \]
is already globally stable. A direct proof in \(z\) would be simpler. We introduced \(F\) in the toy model to reproduce the genuinely nontrivial step in the neural-network proof.
For the full neural network: it is not logically necessary in the sense that no alternative proof could exist. One might instead use:
* stronger norms;
* uniform higher-moment bounds;
* truncation and tail estimates;
* weighted metrics;
* probabilistic rather than worst-case product estimates.
But none of those is supplied by the audited argument, and they would substantially complicate the proof.
For the chosen state metric—normalized \(L^2\) for neuron fields and operator norm for \(W^{(2)}\)—some mechanism that removes or controls
\[ \phi'(z)P \]
is essentially necessary. The nonlinear transformation is the cleanest such mechanism.
So the honest conclusion is:
* the transformation is optional and somewhat artificial in the toy model;
* it is strategically central to the actual neural-network proof;
* the exact cubic form is incidental;
* the real activation requirements are nonvanishing derivative, stable transformed maps, controlled growth, bounded forward activation, and nondegeneracy.

The first thing to establish is what the exact finite state is, and why its width limit cannot be described by a few marginal distributions.
At finite width, define the transformed first-layer state
\[ X=F(z^{(1)}), \qquad F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3}. \]
The complete evolving state is
\[ \Theta_n = \left( X,\, W^{(2)},\, W^{(3)} \right). \]
Everything else is calculated from this state:
\[ z^{(1)}=F^{-1}(X), \qquad h^{(1)}=\phi(z^{(1)}), \]\[ z^{(2)}=W^{(2)}h^{(1)}, \qquad h^{(2)}=\phi(z^{(2)}), \]\[ D = W^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z^{(2)}), \]\[ P = \bigl(W^{(2)}\bigr)^\top D, \]\[ e_n = \frac1n \sum_{j=1}^n W_j^{(3)}h_j^{(2)} -1. \]
The exact transformed gradient flow is
\[ \dot X=-2e_nP, \]\[ \dot W^{(2)} = -\frac{2e_n}{n} D\bigl(h^{(1)}\bigr)^\top, \]\[ \dot W^{(3)} = -2e_nh^{(2)}. \]
This finite system is already autonomous. Given the current triple
\[ (X,W^{(2)},W^{(3)}), \]
we can compute every quantity on the right and therefore determine the next velocity.
The limit must retain enough information for this same computation to remain possible.
The first obstacle is that the distribution of individual entries of \(W^{(2)}\) tells us almost nothing. Initially,
\[ W_{ji}^{(2)}\sim N\!\left(0,\frac1n\right), \]
so each individual entry converges to zero. Nevertheless,
\[ z_j^{(2)} = \sum_{i=1}^n W_{ji}^{(2)}h_i^{(1)} \]
remains order one. The aggregate matrix action survives even though every entry disappears.
Therefore the limiting object cannot merely retain the entry distribution of \(W^{(2)}\). It must retain what \(W^{(2)}\) does to population vectors.
There are two kinds of population vectors:
* right vectors, indexed by first-layer neurons, such as \(h^{(1)}\) and \(P\);
* left vectors, indexed by second-layer neurons, such as \(z^{(2)},h^{(2)},D,W^{(3)}\).
The matrix acts forward from right to left:
\[ v\longmapsto W^{(2)}v, \]
and its transpose acts backward from left to right:
\[ u\longmapsto \bigl(W^{(2)}\bigr)^\top u. \]
Both actions are needed immediately:
\[ z^{(2)}=W^{(2)}h^{(1)}, \qquad P=\bigl(W^{(2)}\bigr)^\top D. \]
Knowing only the separate distributions of
\[ z^{(1)},\qquad z^{(2)},\qquad W^{(3)} \]
does not tell us either action.
The evolution of \(W^{(2)}\) makes this even more concrete. Define the normalized inner product
\[ \langle u,v\rangle_n = \frac1n\sum_i u_iv_i. \]
Then the matrix update can be written as
\[ \dot W^{(2)} = -2e_n \left(D\otimes_n h^{(1)}\right), \]
where the rank-one operator satisfies
\[ \left(D\otimes_n h^{(1)}\right)v = D\, \langle h^{(1)},v\rangle_n. \]
Integrating in time gives
\[ W^{(2)}(t) = W^{(2)}(0) - 2\int_0^t e(s)\, D(s)\otimes_n h^{(1)}(s)\,ds. \]
Therefore, for any right vector \(v\),
\[ \begin{aligned} W^{(2)}(t)v ={}& W^{(2)}(0)v \\ &- 2\int_0^t e(s)D(s) \langle h^{(1)}(s),v\rangle_n\,ds. \end{aligned} \]
Similarly, for any left vector \(u\),
\[ \begin{aligned} \bigl(W^{(2)}(t)\bigr)^\top u ={}& \bigl(W^{(2)}(0)\bigr)^\top u \\ &- 2\int_0^t e(s)h^{(1)}(s) \langle D(s),u\rangle_n\,ds. \end{aligned} \]
These equations reveal exactly what the limit must remember:
1. how the initial random \(W^{(2)}(0)\) acts forward;
2. how the same initial matrix acts backward;
3. the normalized inner products between all dynamically generated vectors;
4. the correlations created by repeatedly reusing the same matrix and transpose.
The same matrix appears in both directions. We cannot replace the backward multiplication by a fresh independent random matrix. Doing so would destroy the correlations and response terms created by matrix reuse.
This is the reason for the action-law state. Informally, it is the joint population description of every finite computation obtained by:
* starting from the current fields;
* applying coordinatewise functions such as \(\phi\) and \(\phi'\);
* applying \(W^{(2)}\);
* applying \(\bigl(W^{(2)}\bigr)^*\);
* taking the required population inner products.
The symbol \(^*\) in the limit plays the role of finite-width transpose.
So the limiting state is not merely
\[ \operatorname{Law}(z^{(1)}), \quad \operatorname{Law}(z^{(2)}), \quad \operatorname{Law}(W^{(3)}). \]
It is the current fields together with the complete future-relevant two-sided action of \(W^{(2)}\).
That is the one genuinely new conceptual object beyond the toy model. The next step is to construct this population action state concretely and write its autonomous flow without assuming prior knowledge of Hilbert spaces.

When \(g\) is differentiable, Gaussian integration by parts also gives
\[ \kappa=\mathbb E[g'(Z^{(2)})]. \]
We must also understand why the projected Gaussian vector can be replaced, for empirical limits, by an ordinary unprojected Gaussian vector. They are not equal: \(\boldsymbol G_\perp^{(n)}\) is exactly perpendicular to \(h^{(1)}\), while \(\boldsymbol G^{(n)}\) is not. But they differ only in one direction:
\[ \frac1n \left\| \boldsymbol G_\perp^{(n)} - \boldsymbol G^{(n)} \right\|^2 \overset d= \frac{G_0^2}{n} \longrightarrow0, \qquad G_0\sim N(0,1). \]
Thus removing the single component parallel to \(h^{(1)}\) has a vanishing effect on average squared coordinates. The two vectors consequently have the same limiting empirical behavior.
At finite width,
\[ (h^{(1)})^\top\boldsymbol G_\perp^{(n)}=0 \]
holds exactly. For the unprojected Gaussian vector, only the normalized version vanishes asymptotically:
\[ \frac1n (h^{(1)})^\top\boldsymbol G^{(n)} \longrightarrow0. \]
In the population limit we no longer use an \(n\)-dimensional Gaussian vector. We use a scalar random variable representing one typical coordinate:
\[ G\sim N(0,1), \qquad G\ \text{independent of }H^{(1)}. \]
A typical limiting backward coordinate is therefore
\[ P = \kappa H^{(1)} + \sqrt{\nu}\,G. \]
To sample this limiting pair, one may:
1. sample \(H^{(1)}\) from its population distribution;
2. independently sample \(G\sim N(0,1)\);
3. set \(P=\kappa H^{(1)}+\sqrt{\nu}\,G\).
Here “orthogonal” no longer means that two finite-dimensional vectors have zero dot product. The precise population statements are
\[ G\ \text{is independent of }H^{(1)} \]
and hence
\[ \mathbb E[H^{(1)}G]=0. \]
It does not mean \(H^{(1)}G=0\) for each sample.
Equivalently,
\[ \mathbb E[P\mid H^{(1)}] = \kappa H^{(1)}, \]
while
\[ P-\mathbb E[P\mid H^{(1)}] = \sqrt{\nu}\,G. \]
Thus \(\kappa H^{(1)}\) is the part predictable from the forward input, and \(\sqrt{\nu}\,G\) is the remaining unpredictable Gaussian variation.
The rigorous empirical statement is that, for every bounded Lipschitz function \(\psi\),
\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \psi\!\left(h_i^{(1)},P_i\right) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\!\left[ \psi\!\left( H^{(1)}, \kappa H^{(1)}+\sqrt{\nu}\,G \right) \right]. \]
This says the empirical coordinate pairs behave, for population averaging, as though we independently sampled \(H^{(1)}\) and \(G\) and then constructed \(P\). It does not say that the finite-width pairs are literally independent.
The next step is to generalize this one-forward/one-backward calculation to repeated uses of the same matrix.
Suppose we have already computed
\[ s^1=W^{(2)}(0)v^1 \]
and now want
\[ s^2=W^{(2)}(0)v^2. \]
Decompose the new input into its component along the previously used direction and a perpendicular remainder:
\[ v^2=a\,v^1+v_\perp^2, \qquad a= \frac{(v^1)^\top v^2}{(v^1)^\top v^1}. \]
Then
\[ \begin{aligned} s^2 &= W^{(2)}(0)(a\,v^1+v_\perp^2)\\ &= a\,s^1+W^{(2)}(0)v_\perp^2. \end{aligned} \]
The first term is determined by the previous matrix use. The second comes from a direction on which the matrix has not yet been observed and is therefore conditionally Gaussian.
If \(v^2\) is parallel to \(v^1\), the answer is completely determined by \(s^1\). If it is perpendicular to \(v^1\), the result is entirely new Gaussian variation.
With several previous forward inputs \(v^1,\ldots,v^r\), write
\[ v^{\mathrm{new}} = \sum_{a=1}^r c_a v^a + v_\perp^{\mathrm{new}}. \]
The coefficients \(c_a\) are determined by the normalized inner products
\[ \frac1n(v^a)^\top v^b, \qquad \frac1n(v^a)^\top v^{\mathrm{new}}. \]
Therefore
\[ W^{(2)}(0)v^{\mathrm{new}} = \sum_{a=1}^r c_aW^{(2)}(0)v^a + W^{(2)}(0)v_\perp^{\mathrm{new}}. \]
Again, this is “remembered response plus fresh Gaussian variation.”
The same principle holds when the matrix has previously been queried in both directions:
\[ W^{(2)}(0)v^1,\ldots,W^{(2)}(0)v^r \]
and
\[ \bigl(W^{(2)}(0)\bigr)^\top u^1, \ldots, \bigl(W^{(2)}(0)\bigr)^\top u^q. \]
Conditioned on these previous results, the matrix has the schematic conditional distribution
\[ W^{(2)}(0) \overset d= W_{\mathrm{known}} + \Pi_{U^\perp} \widetilde W^{(2)} \Pi_{V^\perp}. \]
Here \(V\) is the span of the previous forward inputs, \(U\) is the span of the previous backward inputs, and the projections remove the already-tested directions. The matrix \(W_{\mathrm{known}}\) is the part determined by previous queries, while \(\widetilde W^{(2)}\) represents the remaining Gaussian uncertainty.
Every new forward or backward multiplication therefore splits into:
\[ \text{response determined by earlier calls} + \text{Gaussian innovation in untested directions}. \]
The response coefficients and innovation variances depend on empirical inner products among previously generated vectors. If these inner products converge, then the coefficients, Gaussian variances, and joint empirical laws also converge.
The reused-Gaussian tensor-program theorem performs this conditioning repeatedly and simultaneously for any fixed finite sequence of:
\[ W^{(2)}(0)\text{ multiplications}, \qquad \bigl(W^{(2)}(0)\bigr)^\top\text{ multiplications}, \]
and coordinatewise nonlinearities. It is the rigorous many-call version of the explicit decomposition above.
This is why the theorem fixes the auxiliary Euler step \(\delta\) before taking \(n\to\infty\). Up to time \(T\), there are only
\[ M=\left\lceil\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rceil \]
Euler updates. Since \(M\) is fixed, the complete oracle Euler trajectory contains only finitely many calls to \(W^{(2)}(0)\) and its transpose. The tensor theorem can analyze the entire trajectory at once and identify all its limiting joint distributions and inner products with those of population Euler.
That is the mechanism behind
\[ \text{finite oracle} \longrightarrow \text{population Euler}. \]
The next concrete step is to unroll the finite oracle Euler recursion and verify that every vector produced through step \(M\) can indeed be expressed as this fixed finite sequence of forward calls, transpose calls, coordinatewise nonlinearities, and scalar inner products.

\[ \mathbb E_1 \left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right]. \]
Likewise, propagate the current second-layer error signal backward:
\[ \bigl(W_m^{(2)}\bigr)^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)} \odot\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \]
Substituting the unrolled formula for \(W_m^{(2)}\) produces normalized overlaps of the form
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac1n \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \odot\phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) \right]^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)} \odot\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
Their population counterparts are
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E_2 \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)} \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
This is the exact extension of the toy oracle:
* The toy oracle reads the population residual.
* The neural-network oracle reads the population residual and the population overlaps created by the learned matrix’s rank-one updates.
Nothing else mysterious is being supplied.
The finite oracle still contains:
\[ n\text{-dimensional vectors}, \qquad W_0^{(2)}\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}, \qquad \bigl(W_0^{(2)}\bigr)^\top. \]
But instead of letting its own empirical residuals and overlaps drive later updates, it is fed the deterministic values computed by population Euler:
\[ r_\ell^{\mathrm{population}}, \]\[ \mathbb E_1 \left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right], \]
and
\[ \mathbb E_2 \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)} \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \]
So the three systems are related exactly as in the toy model:
* Finite Euler computes its own empirical residuals and overlaps.
* The finite oracle keeps finite random vectors and the finite random \(W_0^{(2)}\), but reads exact population residuals and overlaps from the public board.
* Population Euler has no finite list of neurons; it computes those expectations from its own population state.
The finite oracle is not autonomous. It is deliberately driven by numbers supplied by population Euler. It exists only to make the proof manageable.
Now the goal
\[ \text{finite oracle} \longrightarrow \text{population Euler} \]
has a precise meaning. Fix \(\delta\), so the number of Euler steps before time \(T\),
\[ M=\left\lceil\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rceil, \]
is fixed. Then, as \(n\to\infty\), we want all empirical coordinate distributions and all normalized overlaps generated by the finite oracle through steps \(0,\ldots,M\) to converge to the corresponding population-Euler quantities.
For an ordinary particle oracle, this would follow from the usual law of large numbers because the oracle particles would be independent.
Here they are not independent in that simple sense: the same \(W_0^{(2)}\) and its transpose are reused throughout the whole calculation. This is precisely where the Gaussian result we studied is used. It proves that every new multiplication by \(W_0^{(2)}\) or its transpose equals
\[ \text{the response forced by previous uses} + \text{the remaining Gaussian variation}, \]
and that all resulting empirical distributions and overlaps converge jointly.
Thus the Gaussian calculation is the replacement for the toy model’s elementary iid law of large numbers.
Once this oracle-to-population statement has been proved, the adjacent arrows are:
\[ \text{finite Euler} \approx \text{finite oracle}, \]
because empirical residuals and overlaps are close to their population values and stability prevents those errors from accumulating, followed by
\[ \text{population Euler} \approx \text{population continuous flow} \]
as \(\delta\to0\).
So the proper next teaching step is not another Gaussian-conditioning calculation. It is to define population Euler and the finite oracle side by side for one update, with the population residual and the two kinds of population overlaps visibly placed where the finite system uses empirical averages. Only after that definition is fully clear should we return to the two-step reused-matrix calculation.

There is a real asymmetry, but it is not simply “first layer versus second layer.” The important difference is:
\[ \boxed{ z^{(1)}\text{ is directly trained, whereas } z^{(2)}=W^{(2)}\phi(z^{(1)})\text{ is computed from the trained variables.} } \]
The raw first-layer flow is
\[ \dot z^{(1)} = -2r\, \phi'(z^{(1)}) \odot (W^{(2)})^\top \left[ W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)}) \right], \]
where \(r\) is the scalar residual.
The problematic part is the coordinatewise multiplication
\[ \phi'(z^{(1)}) \odot (W^{(2)})^\top \left[ W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)}) \right]. \]
The proof controls the normalized size of the backpropagated vector
\[ (W^{(2)})^\top \left[ W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)}) \right], \]
but not its largest coordinate. It may have a coordinate of size \(\sqrt n\) while its normalized squared average remains \(O(1)\). Consequently, a small change in \(z^{(1)}\) concentrated at that coordinate can cause an order-one change in the velocity. This prevents the raw first-layer dynamics from having a width-independent stability estimate.
The transformation is designed to remove exactly that multiplication. Set
\[ x=F(z^{(1)}), \qquad F'(z)=\frac{1}{\phi'(z)}. \]
For \(\phi(z)=\arctan z\),
\[ F(z)=z+\frac{z^3}{3}. \]
Then, exactly in continuous time,
\[ \begin{aligned} \dot x &= F'(z^{(1)})\odot\dot z^{(1)} \\ &= \frac{1}{\phi'(z^{(1)})} \odot \left( -2r\,\phi'(z^{(1)}) \odot (W^{(2)})^\top [W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)})] \right) \\ &= -2r\, (W^{(2)})^\top [W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)})]. \end{aligned} \]
The dangerous first-layer activation derivative has disappeared.
Why does \(z^{(2)}\) not require the same treatment? First, it is not independently updated. It is always reconstructed as
\[ z^{(2)}=W^{(2)}\phi(z^{(1)}). \]
Therefore, for two nearby states,
\[ \begin{aligned} \|z^{(2)}-\widetilde z^{(2)}\|_n \le {}& \|W^{(2)}-\widetilde W^{(2)}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \|\phi(z^{(1)})\|_n \\ &+ \|\widetilde W^{(2)}\|_{\mathrm{op}} \|\phi(z^{(1)})-\phi(\widetilde z^{(1)})\|_n . \end{aligned} \]
Both terms are controlled: \(\phi\) is bounded, \(W^{(2)}\) has a width-independent operator-norm bound, and after transforming \(z^{(1)}\), the map
\[ x\longmapsto\phi(F^{-1}(x)) \]
is globally Lipschitz.
The second-layer derivative appears in
\[ W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)}). \]
But here the multiplying field \(W^{(3)}\) has a coordinatewise bound on every finite time interval:
\[ \dot W^{(3)}=-2r\,\phi(z^{(2)}), \]
and both \(r\) and \(\phi\) are bounded. Consequently,
\[ \begin{aligned} & \left\| W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)}) - \widetilde W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(\widetilde z^{(2)}) \right\|_n \\ &\qquad\le \|W^{(3)}-\widetilde W^{(3)}\|_n + \|\widetilde W^{(3)}\|_\infty \operatorname{Lip}(\phi') \|z^{(2)}-\widetilde z^{(2)}\|_n . \end{aligned} \]
Unlike the first-layer backpropagated vector, the multiplying field here is controlled in maximum-coordinate norm. Therefore this product is stable.
Transforming \(z^{(2)}\) would not produce the same clean cancellation. Differentiating
\[ z^{(2)}=W^{(2)}\phi(z^{(1)}) \]
gives
\[ \begin{aligned} \dot z^{(2)} =-2r\Bigl\{& \|\phi(z^{(1)})\|_n^2 [W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)})] \\ &+ W^{(2)} \left[ \phi'(z^{(1)})^2 \odot (W^{(2)})^\top [W^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z^{(2)})] \right] \Bigr\}. \end{aligned} \]
Multiplying this by \(F'(z^{(2)})=1/\phi'(z^{(2)})\) would cancel \(\phi'(z^{(2)})\) in the first term, but not in the second term because \(W^{(2)}\) mixes all coordinates. Instead it would introduce the unbounded factor
\[ \frac1{\phi'(z^{(2)})}=1+(z^{(2)})^2 \]
outside the matrix action. That would generally make the estimates worse.
So the precise conclusion is:
\[ \boxed{ \text{The transformation is a targeted repair of the unstable } \phi'(z^{(1)})\times\text{backpropagated-field product.} } \]
Keeping \(z^{(2)}\) raw is consistent because its dependence on the state is already stable under the bounds available in the proof. The transformation is not logically necessary for every conceivable proof, but it is central to obtaining the width-independent stability estimate used by this proof.

Yes—your broad picture is essentially right. The precise replacement for the toy argument is:
\[ \boxed{ \text{Hoeffding for i.i.d. oracle particles} \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad \text{a law of large numbers for a finite computation reusing } W_0^{(2)}\text{ and }(W_0^{(2)})^\top. } \]
That theorem controls several empirical averages instead of only the residual. Those errors then enter almost exactly the same stability-and-accumulation argument as in the toy model.
The important corrections are:
* Reusing a matrix is harmless if the vectors multiplied by it are independent of that matrix.
* Reusing it on vectors produced using the same matrix destroys literal i.i.d. structure, even without a transpose.
* The transpose creates an additional, stronger effect: deterministic response terms that would be completely missed by pretending each multiplication used fresh Gaussian randomness.
* In the limit, only empirical scalar averages become deterministic. The neuron vectors themselves remain random population fields.
Let us build this carefully.
Where the easy i.i.d. argument breaks
Let \(W\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) have independent entries \(N(0,1/n)\), and first suppose that \(v\in\mathbb R^n\) is independent of \(W\). Then
\[ (Wv)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n W_{ij}v_j. \]
Conditional on \(v\), different rows of \(W\) are independent, so
\[ (Wv)_1,\ldots,(Wv)_n \]
are independent Gaussians with common variance
\[ \frac1n\sum_jv_j^2. \]
Even several uses
\[ Wv^1,\ldots,Wv^k \]
are easy if all \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\) are independent of \(W\). For each row \(i\), the tuple
\[ \bigl((Wv^1)_i,\ldots,(Wv^k)_i\bigr) \]
is Gaussian, and these tuples are independent across \(i\). The calls are correlated with one another through
\[ \frac1n(v^a)^\top v^b, \]
but this is still an ordinary rowwise i.i.d. situation.
The problem begins when the next vector being multiplied already depends on \(W\). For example,
\[ y=Wv,\qquad u=W\phi(y). \]
Now
\[ u_i = \sum_jW_{ij}\phi\!\left(\sum_kW_{jk}v_k\right). \]
The coefficient multiplying \(W_{ij}\) is itself a function of the same matrix. Thus we cannot condition on \(\phi(y)\) and continue treating \(W\) as an untouched independent Gaussian matrix.
The linear example makes this visible:
\[ u=W^2v, \]
so
\[ u_i=\sum_{j,k}W_{ij}W_{jk}v_k. \]
Different \(u_i\)'s contain overlapping entries of \(W\). They are not independent finite-width samples. Therefore an ordinary statement such as
\[ \frac1n\sum_i\psi(u_i) \longrightarrow \mathbb E[\psi(U)] \]
cannot be justified by directly saying “the \(u_i\) are i.i.d.” They are not.
Same-direction reuse is nevertheless simpler than transpose reuse. If the history only contains calls
\[ Wv^1,\ldots,Wv^k, \]
decompose a new input into
\[ v=v_{\parallel}+v_{\perp}, \]
where \(v_{\parallel}\) lies in the span of \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\). Then
\[ Wv=Wv_{\parallel}+Wv_{\perp}. \]
The first term is a known linear combination of the earlier outputs. After conditioning on the previous calls, the second term is still Gaussian randomness in a previously unused input direction. Thus same-direction reuse produces
\[ \boxed{ \text{known combination of previous outputs} + \text{new Gaussian innovation}. } \]
It is no longer the elementary toy-model i.i.d. argument, but Gaussian conditioning still has a relatively simple form.
Transpose reuse produces something stronger. Take
\[ y=Wv,\qquad u=W^\top y=W^\top Wv. \]
Coordinate \(j\) is
\[ \begin{aligned} u_j &= \sum_iW_{ij}\sum_kW_{ik}v_k \\ &= v_j\sum_iW_{ij}^2 + \sum_{k\ne j}v_k\sum_iW_{ij}W_{ik}. \end{aligned} \]
Because
\[ \sum_iW_{ij}^2\longrightarrow1, \]
the first term approaches \(v_j\). Thus the backward call contains a systematic response aligned with the original input:
\[ W^\top Wv = v+\text{Gaussian-like fluctuation}. \]
If we incorrectly replaced \(W^\top\) by a fresh independent matrix, this \(v\) term would disappear. This is why transpose reuse is not merely “some dependence that should wash out.” It changes the limiting formula.
More generally, after observing previous calls in both directions, Gaussian conditioning gives
\[ W\mid\text{previous calls} \;\overset{d}{=}\; W_{\mathrm{determined}} + \Pi_{\mathrm{unused\ left}}\, \widetilde W\, \Pi_{\mathrm{unused\ right}}, \]
where:
* \(W_{\mathrm{determined}}\) is the part forced by all previous forward and backward calls;
* \(\widetilde W\) is an independent Gaussian matrix;
* the projections restrict it to directions that have not yet been observed.
Applying this decomposition to the next vector produces:
\[ \boxed{ \text{response determined by previous calls} + \text{Gaussian innovation in the remaining directions}. } \]
In the population description, that innovation can be represented by fresh Gaussian variables—but only after retaining every response term and the correct joint covariance between innovations. It is not valid to replace every new matrix call by an unrelated fresh Gaussian.
At finite width, the coordinate tuples are therefore dependent. The reused-Gaussian theorem proves the weaker but exactly sufficient conclusion that, for any fixed finite list of computed vectors,
\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \psi\!\left(v_{n,i}^1,\ldots,v_{n,i}^k\right) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\, \psi\!\left(V^1,\ldots,V^k\right). \]
So the limiting coordinates behave empirically as samples from a joint law, even though the finite coordinates are not literally i.i.d. Within one limiting coordinate, \(V^1,\ldots,V^k\) are generally strongly correlated.
Why the learned matrix can be reduced to its initialization
At Euler step \(m\), the learned middle matrix has the exact finite-rank form
\[ W_m^{(2)} = W_0^{(2)} - \frac{2\delta}{n} \sum_{\ell<m} r_{n,\ell} \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) \right] \phi(z_\ell^{(1)})^\top. \]
Thus all genuinely high-dimensional Gaussian randomness remains inside the single initialization matrix \(W_0^{(2)}\). Everything learned afterward is a finite sum of rank-one corrections.
When this matrix acts forward,
\[ \begin{aligned} W_m^{(2)}\phi(z_m^{(1)}) ={}& W_0^{(2)}\phi(z_m^{(1)}) \\ &- 2\delta\sum_{\ell<m}r_{n,\ell} \left[ W_\ell^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) \right] \left\{ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}) \right\}. \end{aligned} \]
The rank-one corrections enter through scalar empirical overlaps
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}). \]
The transpose action similarly becomes
\[ \begin{aligned} &(W_m^{(2)})^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right] \\ &= (W_0^{(2)})^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)}\odot\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\quad- 2\delta\sum_{\ell<m}r_{n,\ell}\, \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \\ &\qquad\qquad\cdot \left\{ \frac1n\sum_j \left[ W_{\ell,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ W_{m,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \right] \right\}. \end{aligned} \]
So the neural network has three main families of global empirical numbers:
\[ \frac1n\sum_j W_{m,j}^{(3)}\phi(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \]
for the residual,
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}) \]
for forward reuse of \(W^{(2)}\), and
\[ \frac1n\sum_j \left[ W_{\ell,j}^{(3)}\phi'(z_{\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ W_{m,j}^{(3)}\phi'(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \right] \]
for backward reuse of \((W^{(2)})^\top\).
This is the precise generalization of the toy residual. The rank-one vectors do not become deterministic. The scalar empirical averages multiplying those vectors become deterministic population expectations.
The exact boundary between the finite oracle and population Euler
The population Euler trajectory is defined first. At each step it supplies the deterministic numbers
\[ r_m = \mathbb E\!\left[ W_m^{(3)}\phi(z_m^{(2)}) \right]-1, \]\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)})\phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right], \]
and
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ W_\ell^{(3)}\phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)}\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \]
The finite oracle keeps:
* the finite random initialization \(W_0^{(2)}\);
* its actual transpose \((W_0^{(2)})^\top\);
* finite vectors \(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)},\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)},\bar W_{n,m}^{(3)}\);
* all coordinatewise activations and derivatives.
But it replaces the global empirical feedback numbers above by their population values.
For example, its second-layer preactivation is defined by
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar z_{n,m}^{(2)} ={}& W_0^{(2)}\phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}) \\ &- 2\delta\sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
The vector used to update the transformed first-layer state is
\[ \begin{aligned} & (W_0^{(2)})^\top \left[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\quad- 2\delta\sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(1)}) \\ &\qquad\qquad\cdot \mathbb E\!\left[ W_\ell^{(3)}\phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)}\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
The oracle then uses this finite vector in
\[ \bar x_{n,m+1} = \bar x_{n,m} - 2\delta r_m \left(\text{the complete vector displayed above}\right), \]
and updates
\[ \bar W_{n,m+1}^{(3)} = \bar W_{n,m}^{(3)} - 2\delta r_m\phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}). \]
So your proposed rule is close, but the exact rule is:
\[ \boxed{ \text{The oracle borrows the global scalar quantities that are fed back into later computations.} } \]
It does not replace every scalar ever computed. Empirical scalars used only for checking convergence can remain empirical. What must be removed from the oracle recursion are the width-dependent empirical feedback coefficients.
There is one subtle but important consequence. Define the oracle’s finite rank-memory matrix by
\[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)} = W_0^{(2)} - \frac{2\delta}{n} \sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot\phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(1)})^\top. \]
Then \(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}\) is not exactly equal to
\[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)}\phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}), \]
because exact multiplication by the finite rank-memory matrix produces an empirical overlap, while the oracle definition uses the population overlap. Their difference is exactly
\[ \begin{aligned} & \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)}\phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}) - \bar z_{n,m}^{(2)} \\ &= -2\delta\sum_{\ell<m}r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot\phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\qquad\cdot \left[ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(\bar z_{n,m,i}^{(1)}) - \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right] \right]. \end{aligned} \]
There is an analogous discrepancy for the transpose action, involving the empirical-minus-population backward overlap.
This is where the sampling errors appear explicitly. Nothing has been silently equated.
What replaces Hoeffding, and what replaces the toy \(E_m\)
Fix \(\delta>0\) and a time horizon \(T\). Then
\[ M=\left\lceil\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rceil \]
is fixed while \(n\to\infty\). There are therefore only finitely many oracle vectors and finitely many empirical quantities to control.
Call the largest relevant oracle discrepancy \(\varepsilon_n\). Concretely, it is the maximum over \(\ell,m\le M\) of:
\[ \left| \frac1n\sum_j \bar W_{n,m,j}^{(3)} \phi(\bar z_{n,m,j}^{(2)}) - \mathbb E[ W_m^{(3)}\phi(z_m^{(2)}) ] \right|, \]\[ \left| \frac1n\sum_i \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(\bar z_{n,m,i}^{(1)}) - \mathbb E[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) ] \right|, \]\[ \begin{aligned} \left| \frac1n\sum_j \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell,j}^{(3)} \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ \bar W_{n,m,j}^{(3)} \phi'(\bar z_{n,m,j}^{(2)}) \right] \right.\\ \left. - \mathbb E[ W_\ell^{(3)}\phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)}\phi'(z_m^{(2)}) ] \right|, \end{aligned} \]
together with a finite list of squared-norm discrepancies needed to keep all stability constants bounded.
In the toy model, Hoeffding gave an explicit estimate such as
\[ \varepsilon_n = O_{\mathbb P} \left( \sqrt{\frac{\log M}{n}} \right). \]
Here the coordinates are neither independent nor all bounded, so that argument does not apply. Instead, the fixed-program reused-Gaussian theorem gives
\[ \boxed{\varepsilon_n\longrightarrow0\quad\text{almost surely}.} \]
This is rigorous, but it is not an explicit finite-\(n\) rate. The audited proof establishes asymptotic convergence, not a Hoeffding-style \(1/\sqrt n\) concentration bound. Because \(M\) is fixed, a rate is not needed.
The analogue of the toy cumulative state error is
\[ \begin{aligned} E_{n,m} ={}& \|x_{n,m}-\bar x_{n,m}\|_n + \|W_{n,m}^{(3)}-\bar W_{n,m}^{(3)}\|_n \\ &+ \|W_{n,m}^{(2)}-\bar W_{n,m}^{(2)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \end{aligned} \]
This compares the actual finite Euler network with the finite oracle rank-memory state. Stability of all the forward and backward operations gives
\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{difference of first-layer activations} + \text{difference of second-layer preactivations} \\ &\quad+ \text{difference of second-layer activations} + \text{difference of first-layer backpropagated vectors} \\ &\quad+ |r_{n,m}-r_m| \le C\bigl(E_{n,m}+\varepsilon_n\bigr). \end{aligned} \]
Subtracting the actual and oracle Euler updates then gives
\[ E_{n,m+1} \le (1+C\delta)E_{n,m} + C\delta\varepsilon_n. \]
This is precisely the neural-network version of the toy recurrence. Discrete Gronwall gives
\[ \max_{m\le M}E_{n,m} \le C_{T,\delta} \left(E_{n,0}+\varepsilon_n\right) \longrightarrow0. \]
The very small random initialization of \(W^{(3)}\), which has population limit zero, is included in \(E_{n,0}\).
Meanwhile, the reused-Gaussian theorem directly identifies the oracle with population Euler:
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \psi\!\left( \bar v_{n,i}^{1},\ldots,\bar v_{n,i}^{k} \right) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\, \psi\!\left(V^1,\ldots,V^k\right) \]
for every fixed finite collection of oracle vectors and every permitted test function \(\psi\).
Thus the entire bridge is
\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{reused-Gaussian law of large numbers} \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] \varepsilon_n\to0 \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] E_{n,m+1}\le(1+C\delta)E_{n,m}+C\delta\varepsilon_n \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] \text{actual finite Euler} \longrightarrow \text{population Euler}. \end{array} } \]
So your induction picture is right after two refinements:
1. The induction makes empirical averages and projection coefficients deterministic; it does not make the population vectors deterministic.
2. “Fresh Gaussian” means the unexplored part of the same initial matrix after conditioning on its past actions. It must be added jointly with all deterministic response terms and with the correct correlations between different calls.
That is the exact neural-network counterpart of “oracle i.i.d. sampling + Hoeffding + discrete Gronwall” in the toy model.

Yes—your broad picture is essentially right. The precise replacement for the toy argument is:
\[ \boxed{ \text{Hoeffding for i.i.d. oracle particles} \quad\rightsquigarrow\quad \text{a law of large numbers for a finite computation reusing } W_0^{(2)}\text{ and }(W_0^{(2)})^\top. } \]
That theorem controls several empirical averages instead of only the residual. Those errors then enter almost exactly the same stability-and-accumulation argument as in the toy model.
The important corrections are:
* Reusing a matrix is harmless if the vectors multiplied by it are independent of that matrix.
* Reusing it on vectors produced using the same matrix destroys literal i.i.d. structure, even without a transpose.
* The transpose creates an additional, stronger effect: deterministic response terms that would be completely missed by pretending each multiplication used fresh Gaussian randomness.
* In the limit, only empirical scalar averages become deterministic. The neuron vectors themselves remain random population fields.
Let us build this carefully.
Where the easy i.i.d. argument breaks
Let \(W\in\mathbb R^{n\times n}\) have independent entries \(N(0,1/n)\), and first suppose that \(v\in\mathbb R^n\) is independent of \(W\). Then
\[ (Wv)_i=\sum_{j=1}^n W_{ij}v_j. \]
Conditional on \(v\), different rows of \(W\) are independent, so
\[ (Wv)_1,\ldots,(Wv)_n \]
are independent Gaussians with common variance
\[ \frac1n\sum_jv_j^2. \]
Even several uses
\[ Wv^1,\ldots,Wv^k \]
are easy if all \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\) are independent of \(W\). For each row \(i\), the tuple
\[ \bigl((Wv^1)_i,\ldots,(Wv^k)_i\bigr) \]
is Gaussian, and these tuples are independent across \(i\). The calls are correlated with one another through
\[ \frac1n(v^a)^\top v^b, \]
but this is still an ordinary rowwise i.i.d. situation.
The problem begins when the next vector being multiplied already depends on \(W\). For example,
\[ y=Wv,\qquad u=W\phi(y). \]
Now
\[ u_i = \sum_jW_{ij}\phi\!\left(\sum_kW_{jk}v_k\right). \]
The coefficient multiplying \(W_{ij}\) is itself a function of the same matrix. Thus we cannot condition on \(\phi(y)\) and continue treating \(W\) as an untouched independent Gaussian matrix.
The linear example makes this visible:
\[ u=W^2v, \]
so
\[ u_i=\sum_{j,k}W_{ij}W_{jk}v_k. \]
Different \(u_i\)'s contain overlapping entries of \(W\). They are not independent finite-width samples. Therefore an ordinary statement such as
\[ \frac1n\sum_i\psi(u_i) \longrightarrow \mathbb E[\psi(U)] \]
cannot be justified by directly saying “the \(u_i\) are i.i.d.” They are not.
Same-direction reuse is nevertheless simpler than transpose reuse. If the history only contains calls
\[ Wv^1,\ldots,Wv^k, \]
decompose a new input into
\[ v=v_{\parallel}+v_{\perp}, \]
where \(v_{\parallel}\) lies in the span of \(v^1,\ldots,v^k\). Then
\[ Wv=Wv_{\parallel}+Wv_{\perp}. \]
The first term is a known linear combination of the earlier outputs. After conditioning on the previous calls, the second term is still Gaussian randomness in a previously unused input direction. Thus same-direction reuse produces
\[ \boxed{ \text{known combination of previous outputs} + \text{new Gaussian innovation}. } \]
It is no longer the elementary toy-model i.i.d. argument, but Gaussian conditioning still has a relatively simple form.
Transpose reuse produces something stronger. Take
\[ y=Wv,\qquad u=W^\top y=W^\top Wv. \]
Coordinate \(j\) is
\[ \begin{aligned} u_j &= \sum_iW_{ij}\sum_kW_{ik}v_k\\ &= v_j\sum_iW_{ij}^2 + \sum_{k\ne j}v_k\sum_iW_{ij}W_{ik}. \end{aligned} \]
Because
\[ \sum_iW_{ij}^2\longrightarrow1, \]
the first term approaches \(v_j\). Thus the backward call contains a systematic response aligned with the original input:
\[ W^\top Wv = v+\text{Gaussian-like fluctuation}. \]
If we incorrectly replaced \(W^\top\) by a fresh independent matrix, this \(v\) term would disappear. This is why transpose reuse is not merely “some dependence that should wash out.” It changes the limiting formula.
More generally, after observing previous calls in both directions, Gaussian conditioning gives
\[ W\mid\text{previous calls} \;\overset{d}{=}\; W_{\mathrm{determined}} + \Pi_{\mathrm{unused\ left}}\, \widetilde W\, \Pi_{\mathrm{unused\ right}}, \]
where:
* \(W_{\mathrm{determined}}\) is the part forced by all previous forward and backward calls;
* \(\widetilde W\) is an independent Gaussian matrix;
* the projections restrict it to directions that have not yet been observed.
Applying this decomposition to the next vector produces
\[ \boxed{ \text{response determined by previous calls} + \text{Gaussian innovation in the remaining directions}. } \]
In the population description, that innovation can be represented by fresh Gaussian variables—but only after retaining every response term and the correct joint covariance between innovations. It is not valid to replace every new matrix call by an unrelated fresh Gaussian.
At finite width, the coordinate tuples are therefore dependent. The reused-Gaussian theorem proves the weaker but exactly sufficient conclusion that, for any fixed finite list of computed vectors,
\[ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \psi\!\left(v_{n,i}^1,\ldots,v_{n,i}^k\right) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\, \psi\!\left(V^1,\ldots,V^k\right). \]
So the limiting coordinates behave empirically as samples from a joint law, even though the finite coordinates are not literally i.i.d. Within one limiting coordinate, \(V^1,\ldots,V^k\) are generally strongly correlated.
Why the learned matrix can be reduced to its initialization
At Euler step \(m\), the learned middle matrix has the exact finite-rank form
\[ W_m^{(2)} = W_0^{(2)} - \frac{2\delta}{n} \sum_{\ell<m} r_{n,\ell} \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) \right] \phi(z_\ell^{(1)})^\top. \]
Thus all genuinely high-dimensional Gaussian randomness remains inside the single initialization matrix \(W_0^{(2)}\). Everything learned afterward is a finite sum of rank-one corrections.
When this matrix acts forward,
\[ \begin{aligned} W_m^{(2)}\phi(z_m^{(1)}) ={}& W_0^{(2)}\phi(z_m^{(1)}) \\ &- 2\delta \sum_{\ell<m} r_{n,\ell} \left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) \right] \left\{ \frac1n \sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}) \right\}. \end{aligned} \]
The rank-one corrections enter through scalar empirical overlaps
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}). \]
The transpose action similarly becomes
\[ \begin{aligned} & (W_m^{(2)})^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right] \\ &= (W_0^{(2)})^\top \left[ W_m^{(3)} \odot \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\quad- 2\delta \sum_{\ell<m} r_{n,\ell}\, \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \\ &\qquad\qquad\cdot \left\{ \frac1n \sum_j \left[ W_{\ell,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ W_{m,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \right] \right\}. \end{aligned} \]
So the neural network has three main families of global empirical numbers:
\[ \frac1n\sum_j W_{m,j}^{(3)} \phi(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \]
for the residual,
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \phi(z_{\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(z_{m,i}^{(1)}) \]
for forward reuse of \(W^{(2)}\), and
\[ \frac1n\sum_j \left[ W_{\ell,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ W_{m,j}^{(3)} \phi'(z_{m,j}^{(2)}) \right] \]
for backward reuse of \((W^{(2)})^\top\).
This is the precise generalization of the toy residual. The rank-one vectors do not become deterministic. The scalar empirical averages multiplying those vectors become deterministic population expectations.
The exact boundary between the finite oracle and population Euler
The population Euler trajectory is defined first. At each step it supplies the deterministic numbers
\[ r_m = \mathbb E\!\left[ W_m^{(3)} \phi(z_m^{(2)}) \right]-1, \]\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right], \]
and
\[ \mathbb E\!\left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)} \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \]
The finite oracle keeps:
* the finite random initialization \(W_0^{(2)}\);
* its actual transpose \((W_0^{(2)})^\top\);
* finite vectors \(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)},\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)},\bar W_{n,m}^{(3)}\);
* all coordinatewise activations and derivatives.
But it replaces the global empirical feedback numbers above by their population values.
For example, its second-layer preactivation is defined by
\[ \begin{aligned} \bar z_{n,m}^{(2)} ={}& W_0^{(2)} \phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}) \\ &- 2\delta \sum_{\ell<m} r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
The vector used to update the transformed first-layer state is
\[ \begin{aligned} & (W_0^{(2)})^\top \left[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\quad- 2\delta \sum_{\ell<m} r_\ell \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(1)}) \\ &\qquad\qquad\cdot \mathbb E\!\left[ W_\ell^{(3)} \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)} \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) \right]. \end{aligned} \]
The oracle then uses this finite vector in
\[ \bar x_{n,m+1} = \bar x_{n,m} - 2\delta r_m \left( \text{the complete vector displayed above} \right), \]
and updates
\[ \bar W_{n,m+1}^{(3)} = \bar W_{n,m}^{(3)} - 2\delta r_m \phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}). \]
So your proposed rule is close, but the exact rule is
\[ \boxed{ \text{The oracle borrows the global scalar quantities that are fed back into later computations.} } \]
It does not replace every scalar ever computed. Empirical scalars used only for checking convergence can remain empirical. What must be removed from the oracle recursion are the width-dependent empirical feedback coefficients.
There is one subtle but important consequence. Define the oracle’s finite rank-memory matrix by
\[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)} = W_0^{(2)} - \frac{2\delta}{n} \sum_{\ell<m} r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(1)})^\top. \]
Then \(\bar z_{n,m}^{(2)}\) is not exactly equal to
\[ \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)} \phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}), \]
because exact multiplication by the finite rank-memory matrix produces an empirical overlap, while the oracle definition uses the population overlap. Their difference is exactly
\[ \begin{aligned} & \bar W_{n,m}^{(2)} \phi(\bar z_{n,m}^{(1)}) - \bar z_{n,m}^{(2)} \\ &= -2\delta \sum_{\ell<m} r_\ell \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell}^{(3)} \odot \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell}^{(2)}) \right] \\ &\qquad\cdot \left[ \frac1n \sum_i \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(\bar z_{n,m,i}^{(1)}) - \mathbb E\!\left[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) \right] \right]. \end{aligned} \]
There is an analogous discrepancy for the transpose action, involving the empirical-minus-population backward overlap.
This is where the sampling errors appear explicitly. Nothing has been silently equated.
What replaces Hoeffding, and what replaces the toy \(E_m\)
Fix \(\delta>0\) and a time horizon \(T\). Then
\[ M=\left\lceil\frac{T}{\delta}\right\rceil \]
is fixed while \(n\to\infty\). There are therefore only finitely many oracle vectors and finitely many empirical quantities to control.
Call the largest relevant oracle discrepancy \(\varepsilon_n\). Concretely, it is the maximum over \(\ell,m\le M\) of
\[ \left| \frac1n\sum_j \bar W_{n,m,j}^{(3)} \phi(\bar z_{n,m,j}^{(2)}) - \mathbb E[ W_m^{(3)} \phi(z_m^{(2)}) ] \right|, \]\[ \left| \frac1n\sum_i \phi(\bar z_{n,\ell,i}^{(1)}) \phi(\bar z_{n,m,i}^{(1)}) - \mathbb E[ \phi(z_\ell^{(1)}) \phi(z_m^{(1)}) ] \right|, \]\[ \begin{aligned} \left| \frac1n\sum_j \left[ \bar W_{n,\ell,j}^{(3)} \phi'(\bar z_{n,\ell,j}^{(2)}) \right] \left[ \bar W_{n,m,j}^{(3)} \phi'(\bar z_{n,m,j}^{(2)}) \right] - \mathbb E[ W_\ell^{(3)} \phi'(z_\ell^{(2)}) W_m^{(3)} \phi'(z_m^{(2)}) ] \right|, \end{aligned} \]
together with a finite list of squared-norm discrepancies needed to keep all stability constants bounded.
In the toy model, Hoeffding gave an explicit estimate such as
\[ \varepsilon_n = O_{\mathbb P} \left( \sqrt{\frac{\log M}{n}} \right). \]
Here the coordinates are neither independent nor all bounded, so that argument does not apply. Instead, the fixed-program reused-Gaussian theorem gives
\[ \boxed{ \varepsilon_n\longrightarrow0 \quad\text{almost surely}. } \]
This is rigorous, but it is not an explicit finite-\(n\) rate. The audited proof establishes asymptotic convergence, not a Hoeffding-style \(1/\sqrt n\) concentration bound. Because \(M\) is fixed, a rate is not needed.
The analogue of the toy cumulative state error is
\[ \begin{aligned} E_{n,m} ={}& \|x_{n,m}-\bar x_{n,m}\|_n + \|W_{n,m}^{(3)}-\bar W_{n,m}^{(3)}\|_n \\ &+ \|W_{n,m}^{(2)}-\bar W_{n,m}^{(2)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}. \end{aligned} \]
This compares the actual finite Euler network with the finite oracle rank-memory state. Stability of all the forward and backward operations gives
\[ \begin{aligned} & \text{difference of first-layer activations} + \text{difference of second-layer preactivations} \\ &\quad+ \text{difference of second-layer activations} + \text{difference of first-layer backpropagated vectors} \\ &\quad+ |r_{n,m}-r_m| \le C\bigl(E_{n,m}+\varepsilon_n\bigr). \end{aligned} \]
Subtracting the actual and oracle Euler updates then gives
\[ E_{n,m+1} \le (1+C\delta)E_{n,m} + C\delta\varepsilon_n. \]
This is precisely the neural-network version of the toy recurrence. Discrete Gronwall gives
\[ \max_{m\le M}E_{n,m} \le C_{T,\delta} \left( E_{n,0}+\varepsilon_n \right) \longrightarrow0. \]
The very small random initialization of \(W^{(3)}\), which has population limit zero, is included in \(E_{n,0}\).
Meanwhile, the reused-Gaussian theorem directly identifies the oracle with population Euler:
\[ \frac1n\sum_i \psi\!\left( \bar v_{n,i}^{1},\ldots,\bar v_{n,i}^{k} \right) \longrightarrow \mathbb E\, \psi\!\left( V^1,\ldots,V^k \right) \]
for every fixed finite collection of oracle vectors and every permitted test function \(\psi\).
Thus the entire bridge is
\[ \boxed{ \begin{array}{c} \text{reused-Gaussian law of large numbers} \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] \varepsilon_n\to0 \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] E_{n,m+1} \le (1+C\delta)E_{n,m} + C\delta\varepsilon_n \\[-1mm] \Downarrow \\[-1mm] \text{actual finite Euler} \longrightarrow \text{population Euler}. \end{array} } \]
So your induction picture is right after two refinements:
1. The induction makes empirical averages and projection coefficients deterministic; it does not make the population vectors deterministic.
2. “Fresh Gaussian” means the unexplored part of the same initial matrix after conditioning on its past actions. It must be added jointly with all deterministic response terms and with the correct correlations between different calls.
That is the exact neural-network counterpart of “oracle i.i.d. sampling + Hoeffding + discrete Gronwall” in the toy model.
