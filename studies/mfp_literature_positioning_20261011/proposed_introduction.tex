\section{Introduction}

A theory of wide neural networks should support calculations with the
quantities through which learning acts: predictions, losses, hidden features,
kernels, and their responses to changes in the parameters. A useful question
is therefore the following. Given a deep network, a correlated labelled batch,
an observable, and a finite derivative order, can one obtain its mean-field
expectation by a uniform set of symbolic rules? A satisfactory answer should
specify the formulas it accepts, retain the network's actual parameter sharing
and training dynamics, determine every covariance and response coefficient,
and prove that the resulting calculation evaluates the requested limit.

The difficulty is that differentiation changes the probabilistic computation.
Forward propagation, backpropagation and subsequent derivatives reuse the same
random matrices in both orientations. Derivatives of a training observable also
differentiate the residuals and the direction of motion itself. Treating a
reused matrix as fresh, or freezing a moving gradient direction, changes the
answer. Direct finite-network differentiation resolves the algebra but leaves
a random computation whose dimension grows with width. What is needed is a
calculus connecting that finite computation to explicit Gaussian integrals.

Mean-field peeling (MFP) supplies this connection for a declared language of
Gaussian neural derivative programs. Its master theorem connects exact
finite-width derivative compilation to a constructive Gaussian limit;
a symbolic compiler implements the same rules. The language contains coordinate
nonlinearities and their derivatives, polynomial arithmetic, normalized
averages, named matrix and transpose actions, represented parameter updates,
and specified directional derivatives and gradient contractions. It is closed
under fixed numbers of admitted gradient updates and arbitrary fixed-order
initial gradient-flow jets. Thus the observable and derivative order are
inputs to the calculus; the derivation rules do not have to be redesigned for
each new calculation.

More precisely, for an admitted scalar query $O_n$ at width $n$, the construction
produces a finite Gaussian-expectation graph $\mathcal G_O$ and proves
\[
 O_n\longrightarrow\operatorname{Eval}(\mathcal G_O)
 \quad\text{in }L^p,\qquad 1\le p<\infty.
\]
Every node has an explicit integrand and covariance determined by earlier
nodes; the final output is a polynomial in these expectation values and the
fixed parameters. Matrix-reuse responses are computed from the program rather
than supplied as unknown neural laws. Uniform moment estimates justify the
expectation limit, and the construction includes singular covariances and
causal feedback through empirical averages. Compilation of derivatives is an
identity at each finite width; the Gaussian graph evaluates the infinite-width
limit of that identity.

For initial neural response, the calculus gives a sharper description. Consider
a zero-bias MLP with $L$ hidden layers, width $n$, fixed inputs $x_a\in\mathbb R^d$
and labels $y_a$, $1\le a\le m$, with
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 z_a^{(1)}&=W^{(1)}x_a/\sqrt d,&
 z_a^{(\ell)}&=W^{(\ell)}h_a^{(\ell-1)}\quad(2\le\ell\le L),\\
 h_a^{(\ell)}&=\phi(z_a^{(\ell)}),&
 f_{n,a}&=W^{(L+1)\top}h_a^{(L)}/n.
 \end{aligned}
\]
Initialize all parameter entries independently with centered Gaussian laws:
first-layer variance one, hidden-matrix variance $1/n$, and stored-readout
variance $\tau^2$ fixed. Use the stated mean-field gradient metric: mobility
$n\kappa_\ell$ on the first-layer columns and readout, and $\kappa_\ell$ on
hidden matrices, with fixed block factors. For squared loss
$\mathcal L_n=m^{-1}\sum_a(f_{n,a}-y_a)^2$, every fixed-order
initial flow jet of an admitted polynomial observable---including predictions,
losses and feature kernels---reduces to finite sums and products of moments.
Here the observables are assembled from original network quantities by
polynomial arithmetic, permitted averages and contractions, and gradients of
expressions in this same class. No additional nonlinear function is applied to
a derivative field. The moments are
\[
 \mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,\Sigma^{(\ell)})}
       \prod_{j=1}^{k}\phi^{(r_j)}(Z_{a_j}),
 \qquad a_j\in\{1,\ldots,m\},\quad r_j\ge0.
\]
Here $k$ is finite, indices may repeat, and the covariances are those of the
original forward pass:
\[
 \Sigma^{(1)}_{ab}=x_a^\top x_b/d,\qquad
 \Sigma^{(\ell+1)}_{ab}
 =\mathbb E_{Z\sim N(0,\Sigma^{(\ell)})}\phi(Z_a)\phi(Z_b).
\]
This follows because finite differentiation raises activation derivative orders
at the original preactivations; a terminating Wick--Stein reduction then removes
the auxiliary polynomial Gaussian factors. Each remaining activation integral
uses at most $m$ coordinates, independently of depth and derivative order.
The number of terms and required activation derivatives can still grow.
This is the initialization subclass of the general theorem; nonlinearities
applied to updated preactivations require the general Gaussian graph.

The compiler makes these statements usable. A typed mathematical builder records
the network and observable, including shared parameter blocks, symbolic input
geometry and labels. General derivative and jet operations then return the
formula, its Gaussian integrands and covariance tables, and an inspectable
transformation trace. The worked examples cover matrix reuse, singular queries,
represented updates, moving directions, MLP gradient observables and labelled
feature-kernel jets. Generic Gaussian integrals remain explicit; polynomial
specializations admit exact moment evaluation. To request the smaller
activation-moment form, the caller declares the original preactivation nodes;
the compiler rebases and prunes the integration coordinates while retaining
the formal source identities. The distinction between physical
network derivatives and formal source derivatives is enforced in the construction.

The probabilistic foundation is the constructive Tensor Programs calculus
\cite{yang2021tp3,golikov2022nongaus,yang2023tp4b}, including its
feature-learning parameterizations \cite{yang2021tp4}. MFP develops that
foundation into a specified calculus of neural derivative queries, with exact
closure, an activation-moment reduction and an executable realization. Its
scientific value is that a class of nonlinear response calculations becomes a
single mathematical and computational task: specify the observable, expand its
physical derivatives, and obtain a justified Gaussian formula. The relationship
to derivative hierarchies, diagrammatic calculations and existing software is
made precise below.

The assumptions attach to the whole construction. The activation is smooth with
polynomial growth of every derivative; root laws are fixed Gaussian laws, with
independent neuronwise tuples and deterministic initial scalar parameters.
Depth, data dimensions, derivative order, update count and the program are fixed
before width tends to infinity. The theorem gives finite-query expectation
limits, without requiring a convergent Taylor series or claiming a positive-time
reconstruction or a general efficiency bound. These boundaries specify the
calculus for which correctness is proved.
