Independent scoped MFP audit, 2026-10-11.

I identified no substantive counterexample or missing load-bearing step in the actual central theorem within this bounded audit. The manuscript supplies a coherent route from exact finite differentiation to deterministic scalar limits in every finite Lp, including polynomial-growth operations, causal scalar feedback, and singular query Grams. This is a finding about the stated fixed finite language, not certification of every auxiliary calculation or every possible compiler input.

The input boundary was the complete 2,967-line `paper_MFP/main.tex`, complete 416-line `paper_MFP/code/MFP_CALCULUS.md`, required mathematical-presentation/proof skills, and relevant bundled implementation, examples, and tests. I read the complete compiler and expression modules, all three example producers, and selected tests supporting the stated contracts. I did not read the maintained book, other studies, study history, earlier reviews, literature-positioning reports, or Git history. No manuscript or implementation changes were made.

The central statement is narrower than an unrestricted neural-program theorem. In `main.tex:616–692`, widths are equal, named Gaussian matrices connect distinct vector types, different matrix names are independent, root tuples have fixed Gaussian laws with independence across types and from matrices, and every operation is built from normalized averages, coordinate expressions, scalar sums/products, and named matrix actions. Every activation derivative is smooth and polynomially bounded. Program size, coefficients, root laws, depth, data dimensions, derivative order, and update count are fixed before the width limit. There is no coordinate selection, arbitrary tensor contraction, inverse primitive, or width-dependent normalization. Under those conditions, `main.tex:694–728` claims all finite scalar Lp limits, uniform empirical/scalar moments, and same-type empirical W2 convergence in probability. It expressly excludes positive-time reconstruction and limits with growing depth or update count. The appendix's broader finite-second-moment root assumption supports its bounded-derivative W2 theorem; it is not presented as sufficient for the central all-moment statement.

I reconstructed the following links in the proof.

1. Exact finite differentiation closes the specified language (`main.tex:1145–1295`). Writing vector adjoints as \(b_u=n\partial O/\partial u\) explains both the normalized averages in scalar adjoints and the matrix-gradient terms \(b_vu^T/n\). Their Frobenius contractions reduce exactly to products of normalized vector pairings. Current matrices retain one initialized base plus finitely many normalized rank-one updates, whose actions lower to existing primitives. Forming the ambient gradient before state substitution implements ordinary current-state updates. Differentiating the substituted history instead is a different observable, explicitly distinguished in the text. The moving-flow second derivative contains both \(D^2O[V,V]\) and \(DO[DV[V]]\); finite smooth ODE existence suffices to define its initial jets without a common width-independent time interval. The argument does not differentiate a claimed infinite-width limit.

2. The raw-entry estimate (`main.tex:813–933`) genuinely handles dependence between a reused matrix and its input. For an even moment of \(\sum_jW_{ij}z_j\), a tuple with \(b\) distinct indices and \(s\) singleton indices gains one extra raw entry for each singleton through centering and the fundamental theorem of calculus. Gaussian moments and Cauchy–Schwarz bound that tuple by \(Cn^{-(p+s)/2}\), which is at most \(Cn^{-b}\) because \(p+s\ge2b\). Summing the \(O(n^b)\) tuples is then uniform in width. The induction is on program nodes, with all derivative orders proved simultaneously at each earlier node; requiring higher earlier-node derivatives is therefore not circular. The strengthened hypothesis allowing each entry variance to lie in \([0,1/n]\) covers the entrywise rescaling inside the cancellation integral. Derivatives are taken in raw coordinates before evaluation at the rescaled array, so no division by the interpolation parameter or lower variance bound is needed. Scalar feedback and averages are included by the same earlier-node moment bounds and Jensen's inequality.

3. For bounded first derivatives, adaptive Gaussian conditioning is justified by conditional product laws for the residual matrices (`main.tex:2500–2632`). At a call, its input is fixed by the preceding transcript and only the queried residual factor is conditioned. Positive limiting query Grams make the finite regression coefficients continuous; removing a fixed-rank projection of fresh Gaussian noise costs rank divided by width. The source-response formula follows from Gaussian integration by parts and cancellation of the old-forward regression term (`main.tex:2634–2769`). This preserves the effect of transposes and other prior paths through the graph. Source independence is not claimed for the resulting output fields.

4. Singular Grams are not treated by asserting continuity of Gram inverses (`main.tex:2777–2912`). Adding a distinct independent epsilon-Gaussian root to each new query gives each successive limiting Schur complement a lower bound epsilon squared. The perturbed program can therefore use the positive-Gram argument. A finite Lipschitz/operator-norm induction bounds its RMS distance from the original program by \(C\varepsilon\) on events whose probabilities tend to one. On the scalar side, continuity of positive semidefinite covariance square roots, bounded first source derivatives, and finite-second-moment roots pass both values and response coefficients to zero perturbation. Separate formal source slots are kept through this limit. The stated kernel-of-the-Gram identity explains why alternate smooth extensions agreeing on the singular support give the same contracted response even when their individual formal partials differ.

5. Clipping is removed uniformly in width (`main.tex:1037–1142`). The clipped maps have polynomial derivative bounds uniform in their clipping radius, allowing the raw-entry estimate to supply all required higher moments. Input-error interpolation and polynomial first-derivative bounds control coordinate and scalar instructions. The matrix error bound uses operator-norm moments and Hölder, so it remains valid for an input error correlated with that matrix. The Gaussian evaluator has a separate dominated-convergence argument for values, derivatives, Grams, and responses. Convergence in probability plus the previously proved higher moments then yields each finite Lp conclusion. In particular, this proof does not substitute an empirical W2 claim for the stronger uniform-integrability step.

6. Scalar feedback is causal, not an implicit closure assumption (`main.tex:992–1035`, `main.tex:2914–2959`). Replacing already computed scalars by their deterministic limits gives an oracle program; the finite Lipschitz comparisons transfer its limits back to the actual program. Formal Gaussian-source differentiation holds those limiting coefficients fixed. Physical differentiation instead propagates through finite averages. This difference is intentional and supported by separate arguments, rather than an omitted derivative in the physical calculation.

The initialization normal form has a substantial additional restriction (`main.tex:1342–1419`): every nonlinear argument must literally be an original centered zero-bias feedforward preactivation. Such preactivations are Gaussian sources or linear combinations of Gaussian roots because the original forward pass precedes reverse calls. Fields then remain polynomial factors times products of activation derivatives at those arguments. Gaussian integration by parts removes an explicit polynomial factor at each reduction step, even at singular covariance. It does not assert that arbitrary nonlinear functions of derivative fields or updated preactivations reduce to this form. Those remain in the general Gaussian expectation graph.

The compiler contract corresponds to these distinctions. Named objects preserve parameter reuse and distinct source slots; normalized rank-one actions lower before compilation; vector and matrix gradients have the documented different scalings; simultaneous state substitution and explicit frozen seeds distinguish ambient updates from history pullbacks. `mfp_compiler.py:718–791` implements the stated source Gram and response equations, including formal partials before any restriction to singular support. The expression reducer explicitly retains non-flat nonlinear integrals rather than applying a nonterminating Stein reduction (`mfp_expr.py:343–426`). The initialization option checks literal nonlinear arguments and centered Gaussian linear representatives (`mfp_compiler.py:681–709`, `:743–780`). As the guide states at lines 61–72, callers still supply activation regularity and the identification of original network preactivations. Successful compilation is therefore not an automatic proof that arbitrary supplied semantic interpretations satisfy all theorem assumptions. This limitation is disclosed, not a hidden accepted-example gap.

I found no worked example whose claimed central-theorem application lies outside those assumptions. The neural examples use fixed order-one readout variance and shared first-weight columns; `main.tex:1324–1337` expressly excludes the width-dependent small-readout initialization from this theorem. The kernel example's half-mean loss and factorial-normalized jets are stated separately. I checked the reuse correction \(2+1/n\) for identity activation, the duplicate-query response accounting, the moving-versus-frozen second derivatives 120 and 30, and the linear kernel coefficient \(J_2=9\lambda_1\lambda_2\), where \(\lambda_a=\sum_cG_{ac}y_c/2\). These agree with the manuscript. I did not independently reconstruct every coefficient of the large quadratic-activation kernel polynomial. The manuscript itself correctly says that its two symbolic compilation paths share the source-rule implementation, so their agreement alone is not an independent proof of that polynomial.
