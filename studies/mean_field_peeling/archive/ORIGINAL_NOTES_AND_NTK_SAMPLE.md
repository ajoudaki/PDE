# Part I. Original source notes

## 1. Baseline setup

Use a canonical multilayer perceptron with preactivation

$$
z^\ell=W^\ell h^{\ell-1},
\qquad
h^\ell=\phi(z^\ell),
\qquad
h^0=x,
\qquad
f(x)=z^L.
$$

For a single sample $(x,y)$, use the loss

$$
\mathcal L(x,y)=(f(x)-y)^2.
$$

Initialization and scaling are intended to follow the μP mean-field
feature-learning setup. For convenience, all hidden layers may have width
$n$, and the mean-field limit is $n\to\infty$.

## 2. Group $\ell$

Suppose $z^\ell$ is the preactivation generated by the linear weights
$W^\ell$, with activation $\phi$ applied to it. Any object that is a
$W^\ell$ term, a $z^\ell$ term, a $\phi(z^\ell)$ term, a higher activation
derivative, or another scalar function of $z^\ell$, belongs to group $\ell$.

## 3. Explicit terms

The terms above involve direct weights, preactivations, and postactivations
with scalar activation functions such as $\phi$, $\phi'$, and $\phi''$.
These are called explicit group-$\ell$ terms.

An object such as the backward error

$$
\delta^\ell=\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial z^\ell}
$$

is implicit rather than explicit. Chain rules, product rules, and
higher-order derivative rules can expand any finite-order derivative object
into a sum of products of explicit terms. The individual products are called
product terms.

## 4. Scalarization

Product terms are normally matrix or higher-order tensor contractions and
are therefore noncommutative before their indices are exposed. Any tensor
contraction can be written in scalar form by introducing enough indices. For
example,

$$
(AB)_{ij}=\sum_k A_{ik}B_{kj}.
$$

After doing this across a product term, every matrix or tensor product becomes
an ordinary scalar product together with explicit index sums. The resulting
expressions are scalarized product terms. They involve only scalar weight
entries, scalar preactivation entries, and scalar functions of
preactivations.

Because the goal is an initialization expectation, linearity allows the
expectation to be applied term by term.

## 5. Grouping and reordering from bottom to top

Within a scalarized product term, scalar multiplication is commutative. Terms
can therefore be grouped by layer and ordered from the lowest group index to
the highest, with the highest group placed at the far right.

## 6. Pushing the top-group expectation forward

Suppose the highest active group is $m$. The intended step is to integrate the
fresh randomness of $W^m$ only after all lower-group factors have been placed
outside that expectation. Informally, lower-layer randomness is separate from
the fresh randomness in $W^m$.

## 7. Mean-field multivariate Gaussian limit

The forward pass produces layerwise Gram matrices transported by Gaussian
expectations. Schematically,

$$
G^{\ell+1}
=
\mathbb E\bigl[\phi(X)\phi(Y)^\top\bigr],
\qquad
(X,Y)\sim N(0,G^\ell).
$$

In the mean-field limit, each neuron row of preactivations is described by a
deterministic covariance matrix. Distinct rows are independent. Weight
elements are independent unless their indices agree. Weights and same-row
preactivations possess a small covariance controlled by the width scaling.

The intended result is that the relevant top-layer weight and preactivation
coordinates can be treated through a prescribed joint Gaussian structure.

## 8. Wick/Isserlis contractions

If a product contains only linear Gaussian terms, Wick's or Isserlis'
theorem converts the expectation of the product into a sum over pairings.

## 9. Stein/Onsager corrections

If nonlinear functions of preactivations occur, Stein's lemma is used to
separate the weight-preactivation dependence. Informally, this produces a
pseudo-independent contribution plus correction terms involving derivatives
of the nonlinear functions and fewer explicit weight factors.

One product term may therefore become a sum of simpler product terms.

## 10. Weight-activation pseudo-independence terms

Applying the Stein/Onsager step inductively to the highest group is intended
to produce terms in which top-layer weights can be integrated separately from
top-layer activation-related factors, together with explicit correction
branches.

## 11. Wick contractions on the remaining weight elements

Once the weight dependence has been isolated, Wick/Isserlis contractions
remove the remaining weights. Some contraction patterns vanish and others
survive, producing Kronecker or Dirac delta constraints among neuron indices.

## 12. Mean-field peeling

After the top-layer weights have been removed, the remaining nonconstant
top-group objects are functions of top-layer preactivations. The original
scalarization contains large index sums. Wick pairings break these sums into
a small number of equality patterns.

For example,

$$
\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j}\phi(z_i)\phi(z_j)
=
\left(\frac1n\sum_i\phi(z_i)\right)^2
\longrightarrow
\bigl(\mathbb E\phi(Z)\bigr)^2,
$$

whereas an enforced equality pattern gives

$$
\frac1n\sum_i\phi(z_i)^2
\longrightarrow
\mathbb E[\phi(Z)^2].
$$

Each equality pattern defines a family of sums with its own mean-field limit
and width scaling. After evaluating these sums, the entire effect of the top
group is replaced by scalar Gaussian expectations. All remaining stochastic
terms involve lower groups only.

Thus an expectation of scalarized products with highest group $m$ is reduced
to a weighted sum of expectations of scalarized products with highest group
at most $m-1$. This is the mean-field peeling step.

## 13. Repeated peeling

Repeat the same operation one group at a time. Eventually every term is
reduced to products of explicit Gaussian expectations of the form

$$
\mathbb E\left[f_1(Z_1)f_2(Z_2)\cdots f_r(Z_r)\right],
$$

where the covariance of the preactivations is determined by the layerwise
forward Gram matrix and the functions are identity, $\phi$, $\phi'$,
$\phi''$, or higher derivatives.

For a fixed batch of size $B$, the primitive Gaussian integration is intended
to be finite-dimensional and controlled by the fixed batch covariance
matrices.

## 14. Practical tips from the original proposal

### 14.1 Retain only leading width patterns

Only leading surviving width patterns matter for a leading-order mean-field
result. A pattern with fewer free assignments may be neglected if it cannot
recover the lost width order in later lower-layer summations.

The phrase “contribute similarly downstream” is important: local top-layer
counts alone are insufficient if two patterns create different lower-layer
sums.

### 14.2 Recursive scalarization

Full scalarization of every layer at once may be prohibitively complex.
Instead, expose and scalarize only the current top group. After it is peeled,
repeat on the next group.

### 14.3 Recursive states and dynamic programming

The equality patterns and lower-layer boundary signatures created by one peel
may close on a finite family of states. If so, the computation can be written
as a recursive or dynamic-programming system.

## 15. Original headline claim

The original intended headline was:

> Any object involving derivatives or higher-order derivatives at
> initialization of a depth-$L$ MLP in the mean-field regime, with arbitrary
> but constant $O(1)$ maximum derivative order and a fixed batch of $B$
> samples, can be computed through a set of $O(L)$ recursive equations
> involving only explicit Gaussian expectations of layerwise preactivations
> passed through identity, $\phi$, $\phi'$, $\phi''$, and higher derivatives.

## 16. Original feature-learning motivation

Under a μP mean-field feature-learning parameterization, the change in a
hidden-layer Gram matrix after one step can be expanded to fixed order in the
learning rate using higher derivatives of the network at initialization. At
later steps, gradient corrections can similarly be expanded using higher
derivatives.

In the gradient-flow scaling

$$
t\to\infty,
\qquad
\eta\to0,
\qquad
t\eta=\tau,
$$

the coefficients become time derivatives of the training trajectory at
initialization. Each such coefficient is intended to be an object handled by
the peeling program.

## 17. Original conjecture

> For a neural network evaluated on fixed inputs and possessing a well-defined
> mean-field learning limit, any scalar observable such as loss or a
> hidden-layer Gram matrix, provided its training curve is locally smooth,
> has initialization derivatives expressible as sums of products of explicit
> Gaussian integrals. Their covariance matrices are finite and determined by
> the input, output, architecture, initialization, and loss configuration.

## 18. Role of the original NTK sample

The supplied LaTeX note attempted a first-principles NTK derivation using
forward covariance recursions, backward Jacobian moments, Wick contractions,
and Stein corrections. Its intended lessons were:

- forward preactivation covariances obey a deterministic Gaussian recursion;
- backward influence can be propagated recursively;
- the classical NTK is built from forward activation kernels and
  derivative-transport factors;
- diagonal and off-diagonal backward patterns must be distinguished;
- off-diagonal corrections should be checked rather than discarded by an
  independence assumption.

The sample is a source of intuition, not an adopted proof. Its notation
changes weight-index conventions, switches from square loss to a linear-output
surrogate, and leaves several second-moment and off-diagonal states
incompletely defined.

# Part II. Original NTK sample execution

## A.1 Title and stated purpose

**Neural Tangent Kernel: A First Principles Derivation**  
Amir Joudaki, ETH Zurich

The sample described itself as a first-principles derivation of the Neural
Tangent Kernel using the law of large numbers, central limit theorem, and
Gaussian integration identities. It aimed to establish mean-field variance
recursions, analyze kernel shifts through parameter updates, derive the
classical NTK recursion, analyze possible off-diagonal backward interactions,
and argue that these corrections are negligible at infinite width.

## A.2 Network architecture in the sample

The sample used an input layer, $L-1$ hidden layers, and a scalar output:

$$
h^0(x)=x\in\mathbb R^{d_0},
$$

$$
z^\ell(x)=W^{\ell-1}h^{\ell-1}(x)+b^{\ell-1},
$$

$$
h^\ell(x)=\phi(z^\ell(x)),
\qquad 1\le\ell<L,
$$

and

$$
z^L(x)=W^{L-1}h^{L-1}(x)+b^{L-1}.
$$

Weights and biases were initialized as

$$
W^\ell_{ij}\sim N\left(0,\frac{\sigma_w^2}{d_\ell}\right),
\qquad
b_i^\ell\sim N(0,\sigma_b^2).
$$

For the sample derivation, the “loss” was changed from the squared loss in the
baseline notes to the scalar output

$$
\mathcal L=z^L(x').
$$

The stated motivation was to isolate the gradient structure and obtain a
simple first-moment boundary.

## A.3 Forward kernel recursion in the sample

The preactivation kernel was defined by

$$
\Sigma^\ell(x,x')
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\langle z^\ell(x),z^\ell(x')\rangle.
$$

The proposed infinite-width recursion was

$$
\Sigma^0(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2\frac{x^\top x'}{d_0}+\sigma_b^2,
$$

$$
\Sigma^{\ell+1}(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi(z)\phi(z')]+\sigma_b^2,
$$

where $(z,z')$ is centered Gaussian with covariance

$$
\begin{pmatrix}
\Sigma^\ell(x,x) & \Sigma^\ell(x,x')\\
\Sigma^\ell(x,x') & \Sigma^\ell(x',x')
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The derivative kernel was defined as

$$
\dot\Sigma^\ell(x,x')
:=
\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi'(z)\phi'(z')],
$$

with the Gaussian covariance inherited from the previous forward layer.

## A.4 Influence function in the sample

For a parameter block $W^{k-1}$, the sample defined the influence on layer
$\ell$ by

$$
\dot\Theta_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}
\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\left\langle
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial W^{k-1}},
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial W^{k-1}}
\right\rangle_{W^{k-1}}.
$$

Using the derivative of $Wa$, it rewrote this schematically as

$$
\dot\Theta_{k,\ell}
=
\langle h^{k-1}(x),h^{k-1}(x')\rangle
\frac1{d_\ell}\sum_i
\left\langle
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial z^k},
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z^k}
\right\rangle_{z^k}.
$$

## A.5 Backward Jacobian moments in the sample

With

$$
J_j^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
:=
\frac{\partial z^\ell(x)}{\partial z_j^k},
$$

the sample introduced

$$
M_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\mathbb E\left[
\sum_{j=1}^{d_k}
J_{j,i}^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
J_j^{(L\leftarrow k)}(x')
\right],
$$

$$
Q_{k,\ell}
:=
\frac1{d_\ell}\sum_{i=1}^{d_\ell}
\mathbb E\left[
\left(
\sum_{j=1}^{d_k}
J_{j,i}^{(\ell\leftarrow k)}(x)
J_j^{(L\leftarrow k)}(x')
\right)^2
\right],
$$

and

$$
V_{k,\ell}=Q_{k,\ell}-M_{k,\ell}^2.
$$

## A.6 First-moment recursion proposed in the sample

The sample stated

$$
M_{k,\ell}=\dot\Sigma^k(x,x')M_{k+1,\ell},
$$

and therefore

$$
M_{k,\ell}
=
M_{\ell,\ell}
\prod_{i=k}^{\ell-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

Its proof expanded the Jacobian recursion

$$
\frac{\partial z_i^{\ell+1}}{\partial z_j^\ell}
=
W^\ell_{ij}\phi'(z_j^\ell)
$$

inside $M_{k,\ell}$, producing a term of the form

$$
\begin{aligned}
M_{k,\ell}
&=
\frac1{d_\ell}\sum_{i,j,s,r}
\mathbb E\Bigg[
\frac{\partial z_i^\ell(x)}{\partial z_s^{k+1}}
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z_r^{k+1}}
\\
&\qquad\qquad\times
\phi'(z_j^k(x))
\phi'(z_j^k(x'))
W^k_{sj}W^k_{rj}
\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
$$

The source proof then invoked independence of the backward Jacobians from
$W^k$ and used

$$
\mathbb E[W^k_{sj}W^k_{rj}]
=
\delta_{sr}\frac{\sigma_w^2}{d_k}.
$$

It concluded with a derivative-kernel factor times the next backward moment.
The maintained theory does not adopt this independence step without a
Wick--Stein analysis, because upper activation derivatives can contain
preactivations depending on $W^k$.

## A.7 Boundary conditions proposed in the sample

The sample stated

$$
M_{\ell,\ell}
=
\begin{cases}
0,&\ell<L,\\
1,&\ell=L.
\end{cases}
$$

The reasoning was that

$$
M_{\ell,\ell}
=
\frac1{d_\ell}\sum_i
\mathbb E\left[
\frac{\partial z^L(x')}{\partial z_i^\ell}
\right],
$$

which equals one at the scalar output and was asserted to vanish at hidden
layers by independence and symmetry.

The resulting proposed complete first moment was

$$
M_{k,\ell}
=
\begin{cases}
0,&\ell<L,\\
\displaystyle\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x'),&\ell=L.
\end{cases}
$$

## A.8 Second-moment statement in the sample

The sample proposed

$$
Q_{k,\ell}
=
\left(\dot\Sigma^k(x,x')\right)^2Q_{k+1,\ell}
+O(1/d_k),
$$

with boundary relation

$$
Q_{\ell,\ell}
=
Q_{\ell+1,\ell+1}
\dot\Sigma^{\ell+1}(x',x')
\frac{d_{\ell+1}}{d_\ell}.
$$

The proof was described as following from an analogous Wick expansion, with
the leading pairing producing the squared derivative-kernel term. The source
did not completely define or prove all normalization and boundary details.

## A.9 NTK expression in the sample

For a learning-rate scaling of the form
$\eta_k\propto\eta_0/d_{k-1}$, the source proposed the expected layer
contribution

$$
\mathbb E[\dot\Theta_{k,L}]
=
\eta_0\Sigma^{k-1}(x,x')
\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

Summing parameter blocks gave

$$
\sum_{k=1}^L\mathbb E[\dot\Theta_{k,L}]
=
\eta_0\sum_{k=1}^L
\Sigma^{k-1}(x,x')
\prod_{i=k}^{L-1}\dot\Sigma^i(x,x').
$$

The NTK recursion was then stated as

$$
\Theta^L(x,x')
=
\Theta^{L-1}(x,x')\dot\Sigma^{L-1}(x,x')
+\Sigma^{L-1}(x,x'),
$$

with base case

$$
\Theta^1(x,x')=\Sigma^0(x,x').
$$

## A.10 Signal propagation remark

For $x=x'$, the source wrote

$$
\dot\Sigma^\ell(x,x)
=
\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi'(Z)^2],
$$

and observed that the product of derivative kernels controls backward signal
propagation. It identified the edge-of-chaos condition with

$$
\chi_1
:=
\sigma_w^2\mathbb E[\phi'(\sqrt{q^\star}Z)^2]
\approx1.
$$

## A.11 Extended Stein identities in the sample

For jointly Gaussian $(X,Y,Z)$, the source recorded identities of the form

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)Y]
=
\sigma_{XY}\mathbb E[f'(X)],
$$

and

$$
\mathbb E[f(X)f(Y)Z]
=
\sigma_{XZ}\mathbb E[f'(X)f(Y)]
+\sigma_{YZ}\mathbb E[f(X)f'(Y)].
$$

These were intended to control forward--backward dependence missed by naive
independence.

## A.12 Tentative diagonal/off-diagonal system

The source proposed a coupled system

$$
D_{k,\ell}
=
\dot\Sigma^{k+1}D_{k+1,\ell}
+\frac{\sigma_w^4}{d_k}
(\mu_{\phi''}^{k+1})^2
C^k_{\phi,\phi}
S_{k+1,\ell},
$$

$$
S_{k,\ell}
=
2\sigma_w^4
C^{k+1}_{\phi'',\phi''}
(\mu_\phi^{k+1})^2D_{k+1,\ell}
+(\mu_\phi^{k+1})^2
(\mu_{\phi''}^{k+1})^2S_{k+1,\ell}.
$$

Here $D$ was intended to represent diagonal backward contributions and $S$
off-diagonal corrections. The source claimed:

1. off-diagonal corrections are $O(1/d_k)$ or carry activation-mean factors;
2. centered activations make some off-diagonal terms vanish;
3. the classical NTK is recovered at leading order.

These equations remain archival and tentative because their normalization,
state definitions, and boundary conditions were not fully specified.

## A.13 Conclusions stated in the sample

The sample concluded that:

1. infinite width produces clean law-of-large-numbers recursions;
2. only the last layer contributes to the chosen first moment under the
   linear-output surrogate loss;
3. variance contributions vanish at infinite width;
4. off-diagonal backward interactions are negligible;
5. the NTK emerges from parameter-gradient inner products.

It also emphasized that the NTK is independent of the learning rate as an
architectural kernel, while the learning rate enters the training update

$$
\Delta f=-\eta\Theta\,\partial_f\mathcal L.
$$

Finally, it suggested extension to residual networks, normalization layers,
and other architectures by modifying the forward and backward recursions.

## A.14 Current archival status

This source execution remains important because it supplied the motivating
example for the peeling program. It should not be cited as a completed proof
without repairing:

- the inconsistent layer indexing;
- the change of loss;
- raw versus effective weight normalization;
- the dependence of upper backward Jacobians on the current weight layer;
- the definitions and normalization of the second-moment states;
- the tentative off-diagonal recursion.

The checked backward-kernel execution in the maintained case study is the
current replacement for the corresponding independence-based step.
