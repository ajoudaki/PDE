A neuron's history, in two clocks

Ten traces from one training run (two hidden tanh layers, width 512, 16 training points on a circle, gradient flow), read off like electrode recordings: five forward activations on the left, five backward carriers on the right. Along ordinary time t they are a fast transient followed by a long stretch of nothing. Re-parameterised by the learning clock τ(t) = ∫₀t ρ, with ρ = √loss, the same traces become smooth, slowly varying functions — the kind a handful of moments can store.

Each trace is drawn at its own gain, EEG-style, with no axes of its own. The thin grey trace on top is the residual ρ = √loss, the speed of learning. Small ticks above each column mark where the other clock's round values land: in the τ view, physical times 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100 pile up on the right; in the t view almost all of τ is spent in the first third of the run. Forward = first-layer activation ha on one fixed training input; backward = ra δa / ρ, the normalised carrier that the weight update is made of. Same network and seed as the moments figure in the paper, but a harder target — 16 inputs, labels 2(0.9 cos θ + 0.9 sin 5θ) — so that features move visibly; the five neurons in each column are the most active among 512. Euler step 0.05, shown until the residual falls below 10⁻⁶ (t ≈ 113). The sharp hook at the very end of the τ view is the normalised residual direction still rotating while ρ, and with it τ, has all but stopped.
