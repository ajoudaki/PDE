Compressing Global Dynamics of Deep Nonlinear Feature Learning

                                              Amir Joudaki

                                           September 27, 2026


                                                  Abstract
          How much can the training dynamics of a deep network be compressed? Compressing a trained
      function does not by itself compress its learning: networks that agree on every input can drift
      apart once training resumes. We ask how small a state suffices to reproduce the whole training
      trajectory while keeping depth, effective nonlinearity and feature learning, because the state that
      survives is a candidate for the variables that govern learning. History-based descriptions such
      as Tensor Programs and dynamical mean-field theory remove explicit width dependence while
      retaining temporal response objects. We show that the history itself is compressible. Measured
      in a clock that runs at the speed of learning, each neuron’s forward and backward response
      history is summarized by P shifted-Legendre moments. The resulting autonomous system has a
      fixed number of coordinates throughout a prescribed finite horizon. At every fixed width and
      finite depth, under the stated activation regularity, it tracks the whole parameter trajectory
      with error OT (P −1 ), or OT (P −2 ) with a second, joint clock. Pairing forward and backward
      memories makes the compression defect a product of two projection errors. As a corollary, every
      learned hidden-weight increment on that horizon lies within OT (P −1 ) of a rank-mP matrix
      generated online from response moments, where m is the sample count. The initialized matrices
      are retained. Small orders closely reproduce dense fitted predictions in whole-circle RMS and on
      held-out MNIST inputs, and outperform the frozen and directly trained low-rank alternatives
      tested on the circle tasks. Placed on a common axis of evolving state against accuracy, existing
      descriptions of training pay for the limit in neurons, in stored history or in tuples of samples;
      response memory pays in an approximation order.



1    Introduction
A missing model of learning. The Turing machine is the canonical model of computation. It
is idealized—it has no fixed bound on memory—and it is simple enough to reason about, while
physical computers, from the von Neumann architecture onward, are understood as its finite-resource
implementations. One might view neural networks as playing the role in learning that physical
computers play in computation; what this analogy still lacks is a counterpart to the Turing machine—
a canonical idealized model of learning. Deep neural networks trained by gradient descent are the
obvious candidates for implementations of such a model, but the object they would implement is
not yet known. The obstacle is that their training dynamics are enormously complex and have no
simple representation.

Why look in the limit. A natural place to look for the idealized object is the limit of infinite
width. Under feature-learning scalings of the type used in tensor-program and mean-field theory
(Yang and Hu, 2021; Nguyen and Pham, 2023), width-dependent learning rates preserve order-one
feature evolution, and suitable population observables obey deterministic limiting laws. The limit is

Why compression. Going to infinite width to simplify seems paradoxical: the dynamical system
grows with width and becomes infinite in the limit. Statistical physics resolves the paradox in other
settings. A gas of 1023 molecules is simpler to describe than a gas of ten, because its macroscopic
behaviour is captured by a few collective variables; mean-field theory replaces intricate particle
interactions by smooth population fields. Training presents two sources of complexity: the number
of neurons, and the response history introduced when evolving weights are eliminated from the
description. The weights themselves are a Markovian state; response-based limits express their
learned interactions through the past. Mean-field and tensor-program limits address width. Whether
the response history can also be compressed is our question. We do not seek compression for its
own sake. A compressed description must retain what drives learning and may discard the rest,
so the state that survives is a candidate for the variables that govern learning, and a compressed
system that reproduces training would be a step toward the idealized model.

Training, not snapshots. Snapshot compression asks only for a representation of the current
input–output map. Two networks that compute the same function on every input can have different
Jacobians and backward responses, and once training resumes the discrepancy accumulates. An
idealized model of learning must reproduce the whole trajectory.

What the model must keep. Several influential simplifications isolate different aspects of
training. Deep linear networks admit exact solutions but remove the nonlinearity (Saxe et al., 2014);
one-hidden-layer mean-field models remove multilayer hidden interactions (Chizat and Bach, 2018;


                                                                              2
Mei et al., 2018; Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2019); and the neural tangent kernel keeps depth and
nonlinearity but freezes the features (Jacot et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019; Chizat et al., 2019). Each
is simple precisely because of what it gives up. Our target keeps all three: depth (more than one
hidden layer), nonlinearity that stays effective during training and in the limit, and feature learning
(hidden representations move at leading order). Together with a specified population limit, this
contract fixes the object we seek to understand.

The gap. Width compression leaves a further resource question. Tensor Programs carry variables
indexed by every earlier training step (Yang and Hu, 2021), and feature-learning dynamical mean-
field theory (DMFT) evolves two-time kernels whose memory grows quadratically with the number of
time points (Bordelon and Pehlevan, 2022). These descriptions encode learned interactions through
temporal responses rather than explicit learned matrices. This is a substantial reduction in width
dependence, but their direct history and two-time-grid representations grow with the number of
retained time points. We ask whether evolving learned interactions can instead be carried by a fixed
number of neuron-level coordinates, with a bound on the error of the complete training trajectory.

One axis for every description. The gap suggests a way to place all descriptions of training
side by side. Fix a horizon and an accuracy, and ask how much evolving state a description needs
to reproduce the trajectory to that accuracy, and whether that state grows with the number of
elapsed steps. Descriptions that freeze the features, drop depth or drop the nonlinearity target a
different training regime; enlarging their state alone supplies no general approximation guarantee
for our target. The descriptions that keep the contract sit on it, and each pays for the limit in a
different currency: the dense network in neurons, Tensor Programs and DMFT in stored history,
the Neural Tangent Hierarchy in tensors indexed by tuples of training samples. Response memory
pays in an approximation order: one order serves a chosen horizon, and no coordinates are added
merely because more steps elapse. Its required value can depend on that horizon and on the task.
Section 4 makes the axis precise, Table 1 places the descriptions on it, and Appendix E works out,
under stated hypotheses, what each would cost at a prescribed accuracy.

Results. Section 4 makes compression of dynamics precise and distinguishes three classes of state:
scalars, populations and operators. We then show the following.
   1. Exact descriptions and obstacles (Section 5). An exact operator closure for a deep linear
      benchmark and a restricted scalar nonclosure result expose information that simple finite
      summaries can miss. Whether exact deep nonlinear dynamics admit finitely many population
      fields remains open.
   2. Training history is compressible in time (Section 6). For networks of any fixed finite width
      and depth, replacing each neuron’s forward and backward history by P Legendre moments
      in a learning-speed clock gives an autonomous system whose state is fixed on the chosen
      horizon and whose trajectory is within OT (P −1 ) of gradient flow, or OT (P −2 ) with a joint
      clock (Theorems 6.1 and 6.2). The difficulty is feedback: the memorized signals depend on
      the approximation itself.
   3. A structural consequence (Corollary 6.3). Every learned weight increment is, on a finite
      horizon, within O(1/P ) of a rank-mP matrix built from the network’s own response moments.
   4. Evolving memories are what matter (Section 7). Small orders closely reproduce the fitted
      circle function and held-out MNIST predictions. In the circle comparisons, frozen dictionaries
      and directly trained low-rank corrections of comparable size have substantially larger errors.

2    Related work
We organize prior work by where it sits on the axis of the introduction: whether it targets the
specified deep nonlinear feature-learning flow and, when it does, how its evolving state is represented.

Off the axis: descriptions that give up one property. Exact solutions of deep linear networks
keep depth and feature learning but not nonlinearity (Saxe et al., 2014); their infinite-width limit is
a gradient flow on ℓ2 operators added to the frozen initialization (Chizat et al., 2024), the cleanest
example of an operator description. Wasserstein and mean-field limits describe one-hidden-layer
networks as the gradient flow of a measure over neurons (Chizat and Bach, 2018; Mei et al., 2018;
Sirignano and Spiliopoulos, 2019); they keep nonlinear feature learning but not depth. The neural
tangent kernel and lazy training keep depth and nonlinearity but freeze the features, reducing
training to a fixed kernel (Jacot et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2019; Chizat et al., 2019). Each is simple
because of what it sacrifices, and each is exact about a different object. These regime changes can
leave a discrepancy that increasing width does not remove. This concerns the learning dynamics,
not whether a shallow or kernel model can represent a particular fitted function.

On the axis, paying in history. Tensor Programs IV proves feature-learning limits at each
fixed number of steps, with a recursion that includes variables indexed by every earlier step (Yang
and Hu, 2021). Feature-learning DMFT evolves two-time activation, gradient and response kernels,
with memory quadratic and time cubic in the number of time points (Bordelon and Pehlevan, 2022),
and its finite-width extension adds perturbative fluctuations (Bordelon and Pehlevan, 2023). Direct
two-time storage is smaller than dense O(n2 ) weight storage per layer when the number of samples
times the number of time steps is well below the width. Runtime additionally depends on sampled
single-site paths and self-consistency sweeps (Appendix E.3). Multilayer mean-field theory and
Neural Feature Flow evolve fields on neuronal ensembles (Nguyen and Pham, 2023; Fang et al., 2020).
These keep all three properties within their respective parameterizations. They compress width:
DMFT, for instance, has kernel state independent of the number of neurons. Direct history solvers
retain time-indexed state; field descriptions instead retain functions on population spaces. Their
representation and approximation costs must be specified separately. Our result is complementary:
it keeps the neurons and compresses time.

On the axis, paying in tuples of samples. The Neural Tangent Hierarchy (Huang and Yau,
2020) evolves the outputs together with the tangent kernels of order 2, . . . , P , the j-th a function
of j training points, and closes by freezing the top kernel. The hierarchy is an exact identity in
any parametrization, and above order two it captures feature learning; the neural tangent kernel
is its lowestPtruncation. What it pays is combinatorial in the samples: the moving state through

Size against accuracy. Definition 4.1 compares one compression with its reference. To compare
different descriptions of training with each other we ask two things of each: how large a state it
evolves, and how fast its error falls in the quantity that buys it accuracy, its order. Call the moving
state of a description the numbers it updates while computing training, excluding fixed sources such
as the initialization or a frozen kernel. Table 1 places one representative work from each description
of Section 2 on this axis: which of the three properties it keeps, what its order is, how its moving
state scales, and how its error decreases in the order. The entries are orders of magnitude, not a
ranking: constants differ between rows and depend on the horizon. Section 6.6 states what the
theorems of this paper give at fixed width, and Appendix E defines the error metric, states the
hypotheses under which the rows can be compared against the population flow, and works out the
cost of a prescribed accuracy.

Three classes of state.
   1. A scalar ODE evolves finitely many real numbers, independently of width and training time.
   2. A population (or field) ODE evolves finitely many functions, random variables or probability
      laws on fixed spaces—for example the law of a neuron’s weights. Its particle implementation
      stores one value per particle and field; the number of objects is finite, their degrees of freedom
      are not.

                                                       6
    3. An operator ODE evolves operators between population spaces, the limits of matrices. A
       matrix records how each row relates to each column, and separate one-neuron marginal
       statistics generally discard these couplings.
A finite number of named fields or operators need not contain finitely many scalars, so every
compression claim below names both the type and the size of the retained state.


5     Exact compression: operators and obstacles
Before allowing errors we ask what exact capture of the population flow can look like. Operator
descriptions and restricted obstructions to scalar closure motivate approximation without settling
the general exact-compression question.

An exact operator description exists, and is finite only in name. A natural candidate for
an exact description without growing history is an operator ODE: (2) read with the hidden matrices
W (ℓ) as operators. In a deep linear benchmark—one input, three linear hidden layers, order-one
initialization—this can be proved completely: the learned part of the whole network becomes a
single trace-class operator that evolves by an explicit autonomous cubic equation driven by the
residual, and finite gradient flow and gradient descent converge to it on every compact horizon in
the stated limits (Appendix D). One named operator can contain infinitely many degrees of freedom.
Thus an operator equation alone supplies no finite state–accuracy trade-off; such a trade-off requires
a further approximation of the operator.

Current observations generate a hierarchy. For a two-hidden-layer population flow on the
circle (Appendix C), one can describe the dynamics exactly through current observations: joint
laws, on each neuron population, of finitely many expressions built from the current weights, the
initialization, ϕ, bounded products and applications of W (2) and its adjoint. The derivative of each
finite level of this hierarchy introduces higher-level observations. The full collection retains current
information needed for predictions and continuation, but this construction does not supply an exact
closure at a fixed finite level. It does not rule out other finite representations.

An obstruction to bounded-contraction scalar closure. In the same linear benchmark, no
encoder formed from a fixed finite list of tensor contractions of the weights with width-independent
bounded degree admits a polynomial autonomous scalar ODE and polynomial readout that reproduce
the output dynamics on an open set of states at all large widths (Appendix D). The reason is
that repeated differentiation of the output forces ever larger connected contractions: the (2j − 1)st
derivative contains                                                    j

Between the two. Whether the exact operator can be compressed into finitely many populations
is open. Rank-one gradient updates do not by themselves imply a fixed rank bound on their time
integral. This makes exact finite-rank closure a restrictive assumption, but does not prove that
every finite population description fails. The main result takes a constructive route: accepting
controlled error gives an explicit autonomous system with a quantitative state–accuracy trade-off.

6.5    Cost
With L hidden layers, width n, m samples and order P , each learned n × n increment is carried by
2mP vectors of length n. The moving internal state is 2(L − 1)mnP numbers against (L − 1)n2 for
the dense learned matrices; it does not grow with training time and is smaller whenever 2mP < n.
                                                                                                  (ℓ)
The initialized matrices, (L − 1)n2 numbers, are kept as a fixed source. Per step, applying W0
and its transpose costs O(mn2 ) per layer as in dense training, the learned correction O(m2 nP ) per
layer, and the moment update O(LmnP ); the joint clock adds an O(P 3 ) Gram factorization and
O(LmnP 2 ) solves.

Read as a trade-off, Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 say that, at fixed width and horizon, a trajectory error ε

of training samples, and once 2mP ≥ n it exceeds the dense learned matrices; learning from a
continuum of inputs would need a further representation of the input dependence. Nor is this a

training in our experiments.

The theorems hold at every fixed width, for every realized initialization. At the population level
they suggest the following picture, which we state as an interpretation, not a result. For each layer
and sample, the P forward and P backward moments are random variables over neurons, and the

Conditional population cost. If the finite network approximates the population flow at the
root-width rate and the constants of Theorems 6.1 and 6.2 are uniform in width, then the moving

states the hypotheses, derives these allocations, and places the tangent hierarchy and numerical
DMFT solvers on the same axis.

8    Discussion: toward an idealized model of learning
We asked whether the idealized limit of deep feature learning can be described by a small state,
in the way a large physical system is described by a few collective variables. Exact scalar closure
encounters a concrete obstruction in the bounded-contraction class, while general exact population
compression remains open. Approximately, a constructive answer is available. History-based limiting
descriptions compress width; we keep the neurons and compress the history. Measured in a clock that
follows learning, the memory of deep nonlinear feature learning is summarized by a few moments
per neuron and sample, with an error that decreases polynomially in the number of moments and a
state that does not grow as steps elapse within the chosen horizon. An illuminating analogue is the
moment closure of kinetic theory: a finite collection of moments represents a much larger dynamical
object. Here the history moments evolve autonomously, and the feedback of their truncation error
is controlled along the full parameter trajectory.
    Seen from far enough away, every description of training that keeps depth, nonlinearity and
feature learning pays for the limit in some currency and buys accuracy with the matching knob: the
network in neurons, the exact limits in stored history, the tangent hierarchy in tensors over tuples of
samples, and response memory in an approximation order. Regime-changing simplifications answer a
different question, so enlarging them alone gives no general same-target guarantee. This classification
(Table 1) makes the present contribution precise: at fixed width, the evolving learned state is linear
in sample count and approximation order, and its error is certified against the full trajectory
(Section 6.6). Whether the same holds against the population flow is exactly Conjecture 6.4.
Appendix E works out, under stated hypotheses, what each description would cost at a prescribed
accuracy; the reader should take those exponents as the shape of a trade-off rather than as a verdict.
Gradient descent and flow require a time-discretization error allowance when their trajectories
are compared. Population and solver rates require the matched existence, sampling and stability
hypotheses stated there. Our constants have no proven growth law in the horizon or the width, so
“constant in time” is a statement about the state, not about the constants.
    The variables that survive are neuron-level memory variables: for each neuron and sample,
compressed summaries of how it has responded forward and backward, coupled through a fixed
random operator. They become collective only at the population level, and Conjecture 6.4 states
what that would require. Other gaps remain. The initialized random operator W0 is kept exactly;
it plays the role of a fixed random environment, and compressing it while preserving its forward
and adjoint reuse is the main open problem. The constants depend on width and horizon, and the
small orders that work in practice are not explained by the asymptotic rates. We have not ablated
the clock against physical time. Finally, the theory covers smooth activations, and the memory
grows with the number of training samples, so learning over a continuum of inputs needs a further
representation of the input dependence.

The frozen control uses the empirical tangent kernel of the same initialized finite network, including
the learning-rate metric. If J0 (x) is its row parameter Jacobian and D has block mobilities
(n, 1, n), then K0 (x, x′ ) = J0 (x)DJ0 (x′ )⊤ . For the training list X and unhalved mean squared loss,
f˙(X, t) = −(2/m)K0 (X, X)(f (X, t) − y). Writing K0 (X, X) = V diag(λj )V ⊤ , the test prediction is

We evaluate this expression directly and locate the training-MSE 0.001 crossing by bisection, without
